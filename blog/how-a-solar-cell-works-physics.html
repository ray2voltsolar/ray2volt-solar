How a Solar Cell Works: The Physics from Photons to Current

From a photon reaching the glass to current at the terminals: the band gap, the p-n junction, the diode equation and the efficiency limits, with every step worked in numbers.

Four facts to carry away

  • Silicon has a band gap of about 1.12 eV, so light beyond about 1,107 nm is lost, and for a blackbody-like sun roughly 23% of the power is sub-gap and about 33% is thermalised as heat.
  • The open-circuit voltage, Voc = (nkT/q) ln(IL/I0 + 1) with symbols defined below, depends on the logarithm of irradiance: from 1000 to 200 W/m² Voc falls only about 46 mV while Isc falls 80%.
  • Warming raises I0 by roughly 18% per kelvin, so Voc falls by about 2 mV/K per cell, around 80 mV for a cell 40 K above 25 °C.
  • An illustrative M10 cell (13.9 A, 0.70 V, ideality factor 1.1) gives I0 near 2.8×10⁻¹⁰ A, FF near 0.82–0.83 and about 24% efficiency, against limits of roughly 33% and 29.4%.

A solar cell is a diode that happens to be lit. That explains much of a datasheet: why a cell gives about 0.7 V in bright or dim light, why a hot roof loses voltage, and why no silicon cell converts all the sunlight it receives. Many explanations stop at "photons knock electrons loose"; an engineer needs the steps after that.

We build the picture in order: photons and the band gap, absorption, the p-n junction, the diode equation, then open-circuit voltage, fill factor and efficiency. Symbols are defined with SI units before any equation uses them, and every number was computed. You need school algebra, logarithms and one derivative.

The worked cell is illustrative, not a product. Module-level behaviour is in How a Solar Module Works: Cells, Bypass Diodes and the I-V Curve. Here we use CODATA constants and 300 K, where the thermal voltage kT/q (defined below) is 25.85 mV. Standard test conditions use 25 °C (298.15 K), which gives 25.69 mV, a 0.6% difference.

Light as photons and the silicon band gap

Sunlight arrives as photons, each carrying an energy set only by its wavelength. We need these symbols:

  • EE: photon energy, in joules (J) or electron-volts (eV); 1 eV = 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • hh: Planck constant, 6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J·s.
  • cc: speed of light, 299,792,458 m/s.
  • qq: elementary charge, 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ C.
  • λ\lambda: wavelength, in metres (nanometres, nm, in tables).
  • EgE_g: band gap of the semiconductor, in eV.

The photon energy is

E=hcλE = \frac{hc}{\lambda}

In handy units, E[eV]≈1239.84/λ[nm]E\,[\mathrm{eV}] \approx 1239.84 / \lambda\,[\mathrm{nm}], so a green photon at 550 nm carries 2.254 eV.

In a crystal, electrons fill a valence band. To carry current, one must cross the band gap into the conduction band, leaving a mobile hole behind. For silicon EgE_g is about 1.12 eV at 300 K (it shrinks slightly as the cell warms). A photon creates an electron-hole pair only if E≥EgE \ge E_g, which sets a cutoff wavelength:

λc=hcEg=1239.84eVnm1.12eV≈1107nm\lambda_c = \frac{hc}{E_g} = \frac{1239.84\,\mathrm{eV\,nm}}{1.12\,\mathrm{eV}} \approx 1107\,\mathrm{nm}

Light beyond about 1,107 nm passes through, which is the first loss. The second concerns absorbed light. A 3.1 eV photon and a 1.2 eV photon each make one pair, but the blue photon's excess energy is shed as heat within picoseconds as the carrier relaxes to the band edge. This is thermalisation. Each absorbed photon yields at most EgE_g, and the wasted fraction is

thermalisation loss fraction=E−EgE(E≥Eg)\text{thermalisation loss fraction} = \frac{E - E_g}{E} \qquad (E \ge E_g)

Counting charge gives a useful check: one electron per photon means 800 nm light yields at most qλ/(hc)=0.645q\lambda/(hc) = 0.645 A/W. An M10 cell receives 1000 W/m² × 0.033124 m² = 33.1 W at one sun and delivers 13.9 A, about 0.42 A/W, which is plausible after the losses above.

Absorption and why wafers are about 170 µm thick

Above-gap photons are absorbed at different depths. The Beer–Lambert law is usually written I(x)=I0e−αxI(x) = I_0 e^{-\alpha x}, but this post needs I0I_0 for the diode saturation current, so we write the light intensity as SS:

  • S(x)S(x): light intensity at depth xx in the silicon, in W/m².
  • S0S_0: intensity just inside the surface, in W/m².
  • α\alpha: absorption coefficient at that wavelength, in cm⁻¹ (1 cm⁻¹ = 100 m⁻¹).
  • xx: depth below the surface, in cm.

Assume a thin slice dx\mathrm{d}x absorbs a fixed fraction αdx\alpha\,\mathrm{d}x of what reaches it, so dS=−αSdx\mathrm{d}S = -\alpha S\,\mathrm{d}x. Integrating gives

S(x)=S0e−αxS(x) = S_0\, e^{-\alpha x}

Intensity falls to 37% of S0S_0 at depth 1/α1/\alpha. The fraction absorbed by a wafer of thickness WW in a single pass is

fabs=1−e−αWf_{\text{abs}} = 1 - e^{-\alpha W}

Absorption falls steeply toward 1,100 nm because silicon has an indirect gap. The coefficients below are rounded room-temperature values and only approximate. Treat them as orders of magnitude.

Blue light is absorbed within a micrometre; near-infrared light needs a path of the order of the wafer thickness. A thicker wafer catches more 900–1,100 nm light but costs more silicon and needs a longer carrier diffusion length. A thinner one saves silicon but breaks more often. The compromise is roughly 150–180 µm, trending thinner.

Light trapping eases the trade-off. Etching the surface into random pyramids bends light obliquely and keeps it reflecting inside, so the path exceeds the thickness. A tenfold longer path turns the 1,000 nm absorption from 64% into 1 − e−10.2, or 99.996%. The ideal ceiling is about 4n², roughly 49 times for a refractive index n near 3.5; real cells get a fraction of that. A silicon nitride coating about 75 nm thick (a quarter wavelength at 600 nm, index near 2) cuts reflection from roughly a third on bare silicon to a few percent.

Doping and the p-n junction

Electrons, holes and doping

Pure silicon has few free carriers. Define:

  • nn, pp: free electron and hole concentrations, in cm⁻³.
  • nin_i: intrinsic carrier concentration, in cm⁻³: about 1×10¹⁰ cm⁻³ at 300 K (approximate; silicon has about 5×10²² atoms per cm³).
  • NDN_D, NAN_A: donor and acceptor doping concentrations, in cm⁻³.

Phosphorus has five valence electrons. Four bond with neighbours and the fifth is almost free, giving n-type material with n≈NDn \approx N_D. Boron has three, so each atom leaves a hole, giving p-type material with p≈NAp \approx N_A. At 300 K nearly all dopants are ionised. At thermal equilibrium the mass-action law holds:

np=ni2n\,p = n_i^{2}

Worked example: a p-type base with NA=1016cm−3N_A = 10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}, one boron atom per five million silicon atoms. Holes number p≈1016cm−3p \approx 10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}} and the minority electrons number

n=ni2NA=(1010)21016=104cm−3n = \frac{n_i^{2}}{N_A} = \frac{(10^{10})^{2}}{10^{16}} = 10^{4}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}

Sanity check: doping raised holes a millionfold and cut electrons by the same factor. Since ni2n_i^2 rises steeply with temperature, this product is the root of the temperature behaviour derived later.

The p-n junction and the built-in potential

Join an n-type layer (the thin phosphorus emitter, on a p-type wafer) to the p-type base. Electrons diffuse from the n side and holes from the p side, leaving fixed ionised dopants behind: positive donors on the n side, negative acceptors on the p side. This layer, nearly empty of mobile carriers, is the depletion region. Its electric field points from n to p and pushes electrons and holes back where they came from. That push is drift.

Equilibrium is reached when drift cancels diffusion for each carrier. Balancing the electron currents gives the potential across the junction. With kk the Boltzmann constant (1.380649×10⁻²³ J/K), TT the absolute temperature (K) and qq the elementary charge, the thermal voltage is kT/q=25.85kT/q = 25.85 mV at 300 K, and the built-in potential VbiV_{bi}, in volts, is

Vbi=kTqln⁡(NANDni2)V_{bi} = \frac{kT}{q}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{N_A N_D}{n_i^{2}}\right)

This assumes full ionisation and non-degenerate doping. Worked example: NA=1016cm−3N_A = 10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}} and ND=1019cm−3N_D = 10^{19}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}, a uniform idealisation of a real diffused emitter.

Vbi=0.025852V×ln⁡(1016×10191020)=0.025852V×34.54=0.893VV_{bi} = 0.025852\,\mathrm{V} \times \ln\!\left(\frac{10^{16}\times 10^{19}}{10^{20}}\right) = 0.025852\,\mathrm{V} \times 34.54 = 0.893\,\mathrm{V}

Sanity check: VbiV_{bi} lies between the 0.70 V open-circuit voltage used below and the 1.12 V band gap, as it must, because the open-circuit voltage cannot exceed the built-in potential. The depletion width is about 0.34 µm for these doping levels, almost all on the lightly doped side. Any pair formed there is swept apart, electrons to the n side and holes to the p side. That separation is the photovoltaic effect. Pairs formed deeper must first diffuse to the junction within their lifetime, so material quality matters.

The diode equation and the single-diode model

In the dark the junction is an ordinary diode. Forward bias VV lowers the barrier from qVbiqV_{bi} to q(Vbi−V)q(V_{bi} - V), and the minority carriers injected across rise by a factor eqV/kTe^{qV/kT}, then recombine. Shockley's analysis gives the ideal diode equation. Symbols:

  • II: current out of the positive terminal, in amperes, counted positive in the direction light drives it.
  • VV: terminal voltage, in volts.
  • I0I_0: dark saturation current, in amperes, the diode's reverse leakage.
  • nn: ideality factor, dimensionless: 1 when recombination happens outside the depletion region, rising toward 2 when it happens inside. Good silicon cells sit near 1.
  • ILI_L: light-generated current, in amperes.
  • RsR_s, RshR_{sh}: series and shunt resistance, in ohms.
Idiode=I0[exp⁡(qVnkT)−1]I_{\text{diode}} = I_0\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{qV}{nkT}\right) - 1\right]

For long, uniform regions I0=qAni2(DnLnNA+DpLpND)I_0 = qA\,n_i^2\left(\dfrac{D_n}{L_n N_A} + \dfrac{D_p}{L_p N_D}\right), where AA is the junction area and DD and LL are the minority-carrier diffusion coefficient and length. Real cells have thin, passivated regions, so this shows the structure of I0I_0, not its value. Two lessons hold: I0I_0 is proportional to ni2n_i^2, and better material gives smaller I0I_0.

Now add light. Assume superposition: illumination adds a photocurrent ILI_L independent of voltage. This is an approximation, since collection weakens slightly near maximum power, but it serves most engineering use. The illuminated cell is

I=IL−I0[exp⁡(qVnkT)−1]I = I_L - I_0\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{qV}{nkT}\right) - 1\right]

Real cells also have series resistance RsR_s (fingers, busbars, contacts, bulk) and a leakage path RshR_{sh} (edges, defects). The diode then sees V+IRsV + IR_s, and the shunt carries (V+IRs)/Rsh(V + IR_s)/R_{sh}. The single-diode model is

I=IL−I0[exp⁡(q(V+IRs)nkT)−1]−V+IRsRshI = I_L - I_0\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{q(V + IR_s)}{nkT}\right) - 1\right] - \frac{V + IR_s}{R_{sh}}

It has five parameters and II on both sides, so it is solved by iteration. The ideal cell has Rs=0R_s = 0 and Rsh→∞R_{sh} \to \infty.

Short-circuit current and open-circuit voltage

Two points have closed forms. For short circuit, set V=0V = 0 in the ideal equation; the exponential becomes 1 and the diode term vanishes:

Isc=IL−I0[1−1]=ILI_{sc} = I_L - I_0\,[1 - 1] = I_L

With series resistance, the diode sees IRsIR_s. For the cell below that is 13.9 A × 0.6 mΩ = 8.3 mV, giving a diode term of 2.84×10⁻¹⁰ A × (e0.293 − 1) ≈ 1×10⁻¹⁰ A, negligible against 13.9 A. So Isc≈ILI_{sc} \approx I_L, and since ILI_L follows photon flux, IscI_{sc} is proportional to irradiance.

For open circuit, set I=0I = 0. No current crosses RsR_s, so it drops out. Neglecting the shunt path:

0=IL−I0[exp⁡(VocnkT/q)−1]0 = I_L - I_0\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{V_{oc}}{nkT/q}\right) - 1\right]
Voc=nkTqln⁡(ILI0+1)V_{oc} = \frac{nkT}{q}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{I_L}{I_0} + 1\right)

Physically, VocV_{oc} is where the diode's forward current has grown to cancel the photocurrent. Because ILI_L is about ten orders of magnitude above I0I_0, the "+1" is negligible. With ILI_L proportional to irradiance GG (W/m²), two irradiances G1G_1 and G2G_2 at the same temperature differ by

Voc,2−Voc,1=nkTqln⁡(G2G1)V_{oc,2} - V_{oc,1} = \frac{nkT}{q}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{G_2}{G_1}\right)

Worked example, with n=1.1n = 1.1 at 300 K so nkT/q=28.44nkT/q = 28.44 mV, from 1000 to 200 W/m²:

ΔVoc=28.44mV×ln⁡(0.2)=28.44mV×(−1.609)=−45.8mV\Delta V_{oc} = 28.44\,\mathrm{mV} \times \ln(0.2) = 28.44\,\mathrm{mV} \times (-1.609) = -45.8\,\mathrm{mV}

Voltage falls from 0.700 V to 0.654 V, a 6.5% loss, while current falls 80%. Sanity check: each tenfold change in light moves VocV_{oc} by 28.44 mV × ln 10 = 65.5 mV. This is why a module shows most of its open-circuit voltage at dawn or under cloud, and why inverter start-up and string design lean on voltage. The temperature side is in String Sizing for Solar Inverters.

Maximum power, fill factor and efficiency

Power P=V×IP = V \times I is zero at both ends of the curve, so there is a maximum between them. For the ideal cell, write a=nkT/qa = nkT/q (volts). Then I=IL−I0(eV/a−1)I = I_L - I_0(e^{V/a} - 1) and dI/dV=−(I0/a)eV/a\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}V = -(I_0/a)\,e^{V/a}. The maximum is where the derivative of power is zero:

dPdV=I+VdIdV=0⇒Imp=VmpaI0eVmp/a\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}V} = I + V\,\frac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad I_{mp} = \frac{V_{mp}}{a}\, I_0\, e^{V_{mp}/a}

Substituting into the cell equation with x=Vmp/ax = V_{mp}/a gives IL+I0=I0ex(1+x)I_L + I_0 = I_0\,e^{x}(1 + x). Taking logarithms and using ln⁡(IL/I0+1)=Voc/a\ln(I_L/I_0 + 1) = V_{oc}/a leaves

Vmp=Voc−aln⁡(1+Vmpa)V_{mp} = V_{oc} - a\,\ln\!\left(1 + \frac{V_{mp}}{a}\right)

Repeated substitution converges in three or four steps. VmpV_{mp} sits a few multiples of aa below VocV_{oc}. Controllers that hold a string at this point, covered in MPPT Explained, search for exactly the condition dP/dV=0\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}V = 0.

The fill factor compares maximum power with the rectangle Voc×IscV_{oc} \times I_{sc}; efficiency compares it with the light on the cell. With GG in W/m² and cell area AA in m²:

FF=PmpVocIscη=PmpGA=FFVocIscGAFF = \frac{P_{mp}}{V_{oc}\,I_{sc}} \qquad\qquad \eta = \frac{P_{mp}}{G\,A} = \frac{FF\,V_{oc}\,I_{sc}}{G\,A}

No exact closed form for the ideal fill factor exists, but Green's empirical approximation is very good. With the normalised voltage voc=Voc/(nkT/q)v_{oc} = V_{oc}/(nkT/q) (dimensionless), for an ideal cell without series or shunt loss:

FF0≈voc−ln⁡(voc+0.72)voc+1FF_0 \approx \frac{v_{oc} - \ln(v_{oc} + 0.72)}{v_{oc} + 1}

Step 4: temperature

Now vary cell temperature at fixed irradiance. The key is I0I_0, which depends on ni2n_i^2 and so on temperature far more strongly than kT/qkT/q does. For the diffusion-dominated case (n=1n = 1), a standard form is I0=CTγe−Eg0/(kT)I_0 = C\,T^{\gamma}\,e^{-E_{g0}/(kT)}, where CC is a constant, γ≈3\gamma \approx 3, and Eg0≈1.2E_{g0} \approx 1.2 eV is the gap extrapolated linearly to 0 K. Substituting into Voc≈(kT/q)ln⁡(IL/I0)V_{oc} \approx (kT/q)\ln(I_L/I_0):

Voc=Eg0q−kTqln⁡(CTγIL)V_{oc} = \frac{E_{g0}}{q} - \frac{kT}{q}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{C\,T^{\gamma}}{I_L}\right)

The first term is fixed and the second grows with TT. Differentiating, and using the equation itself to eliminate the logarithm, gives

dVocdT=−Eg0/q−Voc+γkT/qT+kTq1ILdILdT\frac{\mathrm{d}V_{oc}}{\mathrm{d}T} = -\,\frac{E_{g0}/q - V_{oc} + \gamma\,kT/q}{T} \; + \; \frac{kT}{q}\,\frac{1}{I_L}\frac{\mathrm{d}I_L}{\mathrm{d}T}

The sign is negative for a clear physical reason. As the cell warms, I0I_0 grows about 18% per kelvin (it doubles every 4 K or so), the diode leaks more, and the voltage at which its current equals ILI_L falls. With Voc=0.70V_{oc} = 0.70 V, γ=3\gamma = 3 and kT/q=0.02585kT/q = 0.02585 V:

dVocdT=−1.20−0.70+3×0.02585300K=−1.93mV/K\frac{\mathrm{d}V_{oc}}{\mathrm{d}T} = -\,\frac{1.20 - 0.70 + 3 \times 0.02585}{300\,\mathrm{K}} = -1.93\,\mathrm{mV/K}

The photocurrent term adds only about +0.01 mV/K. This matches the datasheet-style figure of about −2 mV/K per cell, or −0.29%/K of 0.70 V. For a cell 40 K above 25 °C, as a hot rooftop module commonly runs:

ΔVoc≈−2mV/K×40K=−80mV⇒Voc≈0.62V\Delta V_{oc} \approx -2\,\mathrm{mV/K} \times 40\,\mathrm{K} = -80\,\mathrm{mV} \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_{oc} \approx 0.62\,\mathrm{V}

That is an 11% voltage loss, and typical datasheet power coefficients of −0.3 to −0.35 %/K cost a similar share of power. The derivation assumes n=1n = 1; a slightly higher nn shifts the result only a little.

Limits: where the rest of the sunlight goes

The strictest answer is the detailed-balance calculation of Shockley and Queisser (1961). It assumes a single junction, one pair per absorbed above-gap photon, complete collection, and only unavoidable radiative recombination. Even a perfect cell at 300 K must emit light, which pulls VocV_{oc} below Eg/qE_g/q. A low gap gives much current but little voltage, a high gap the reverse, and the product peaks near 1.34 eV at about 33% for the AM1.5G spectrum. Silicon's 1.12 eV lies close to this flat optimum. The 1961 paper used a blackbody sun, so quoted peaks range from about 30% to 34%.

Silicon has an extra intrinsic loss, Auger recombination, in which an electron and hole recombine and hand their energy to a third carrier. Including it, Richter, Hermle and Glunz (2013) calculated an upper limit of about 29.4% for a silicon cell. The budget below shows the scale. The first two rows come from the blackbody integration, so figures are approximate and row 3 is found by difference.

How PERC, TOPCon and HJT attack recombination

The saturation current I0I_0 sums all recombination in the cell, so lowering it raises VocV_{oc}. If I0I_0 falls to I0′I_0' at the same light and temperature, the voltage gain is

ΔVoc=nkTqln⁡(I0I0′)\Delta V_{oc} = \frac{nkT}{q}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{I_0}{I_0'}\right)

For the example cell, cutting I0I_0 threefold adds 28.44 mV × ln 3 = 31.2 mV; halving it adds 19.7 mV. Bulk, surface and contact recombination all add to I0I_0, and a bare metal-silicon interface is among the worst. Each design below answers one question: how do you extract current without recombination at the metal?

  • PERC adds a dielectric layer (commonly aluminium oxide with silicon nitride) to the rear surface. Metal touches the silicon only through small local openings, which cuts rear-surface recombination.
  • TOPCon places an ultrathin tunnel oxide, a nanometre or two thick, and doped polysilicon under the metal. Carriers tunnel to the contact while minority carriers are blocked, which strongly reduces contact recombination. Cells are generally built on n-type wafers.
  • HJT (heterojunction) coats both wafer surfaces with a few nanometres of amorphous silicon. Thin intrinsic layers passivate the surface, and doped layers form the junction and contacts. It is processed at low temperature.

All three target the same loss: surface and contact recombination, which sets VocV_{oc} and fill factor. For how these technologies appear on quotes and datasheets, including bifacial gain, see Solar Panel Types Explained: Mono PERC, TOPCon, and Bifacial.

Frequently asked questions

Why does a solar cell still give voltage in weak light?

The open-circuit voltage depends on the logarithm of the light-generated current. Cutting irradiance from 1000 to 200 W/m² reduces current by 80% but lowers the voltage of a typical silicon cell by only about 46 mV, from 0.70 V to 0.65 V. Power falls almost in proportion to light, while voltage stays close to full.

Why does a hot solar panel produce less power?

The cell saturation current grows by about 18% per kelvin, so the diode leaks more and the voltage at which it cancels the photocurrent falls. A typical silicon cell loses about 2 mV per kelvin, roughly 80 mV for a cell 40 K above 25 °C. Datasheet power coefficients of about 0.3 to 0.35 percent per kelvin follow from this.

What limits the efficiency of a silicon solar cell?

Photons below the band gap pass through, photons above it lose their excess energy as heat, and radiative recombination is unavoidable. Shockley and Queisser (1961) put the single-junction ceiling near 33 percent, and Richter, Hermle and Glunz (2013) calculated about 29.4 percent for silicon with Auger recombination. Commercial cells reach the mid-20s percent.

Why is a single solar cell only about 0.7 V?

Cell voltage is set by the silicon band gap of about 1.12 eV and by recombination, and it cannot exceed the built-in potential of the junction. A larger cell gives more current, not more voltage. Modules reach higher voltages by connecting many cells in series, so the string voltage is the sum of cell voltages.

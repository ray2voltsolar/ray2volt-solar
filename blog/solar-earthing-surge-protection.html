Earthing and Surge Protection for Solar Plants: The Physics of Fault Currents and Lightning

How a fault becomes a touch voltage, how soil sets electrode resistance, and why a wiring loop or a metre of lead can add kilovolts. Worked numbers throughout.

Four numbers to carry away

  • A 230 V fault with an illustrative loop impedance of 0.8 Ω drives about 290 A and trips a breaker almost at once. A PV string is limited to about its short-circuit current, near 11 A in our example, so it cannot trip a breaker the same way.
  • A 3 m driven rod of 16 mm diameter has about 33 Ω in 100 Ω·m soil and about 100 Ω in 300 Ω·m soil. Resistance scales with soil resistivity.
  • A 30 kA strike 50 m away, rising in 1 µs, induces about 1.1 kV in a 10 m² wiring loop but only about 12 V in a 0.1 m² loop.
  • One metre of surge-protector lead adds roughly 10 kV when current changes at 10 kA per microsecond. Keep leads short.

A solar plant is a large metal structure bolted to your building, wired with live DC cables and left outdoors through every thunderstorm season. Two threats follow. Internally, insulation fails and someone touches a live frame. Externally, lightning or a grid switching event drives a fast current into the structure and the wiring.

Earthing answers the first threat and surge protection the second. Both rest on Ohm’s law, on how current spreads through soil and on the voltage a changing current produces across an inductance. This guide defines every symbol before using it and works clearly labelled illustrative numbers. It is an explanation, not a design method. For questions to ask before you buy, see our guide to choosing a solar installer.

Qualified electricians only: All earthing, bonding and surge-protection design and work must be done by qualified, licensed electricians, to the applicable standards (IS 3043, IS 732, IEC 60364-7-712, IEC 62305, IEC 61643-31 and IEC 61643-32) and the CEA safety regulations. PV strings are live whenever light falls on them, and opening the DC isolator does not de-energise the cables on the array side. The values here are illustrative. Do not use them to design, test or modify a plant.

Why earthing exists: touch voltage and the fault loop

When insulation fails, a live conductor can touch an exposed metal part such as a module frame or an inverter enclosure. Earthing gives the fault a low-resistance return path, so the metal stays near earth potential and the current is large enough to trip a protective device.

Touch voltage and body current

  • VtV_t is the touch voltage in volts (V) between the exposed metal and the ground under a person’s feet.
  • IfI_f is the fault current in amperes (A).
  • RpathR_{\text{path}} is the resistance in ohms (Ω) of the protective path between the touched metal and the earth reference.
  • RbodyR_{\text{body}} is the resistance of the body path, including skin contact, in ohms.
  • IbodyI_{\text{body}} is the current through the person, in amperes.

Assume steady current and feet at reference-earth potential. Ohm’s law gives the touch voltage across the path, and the body current across the body.

Vt≈IfRpathV_t \approx I_f \, R_{\text{path}}
Ibody=VtRbodyI_{\text{body}} = \frac{V_t}{R_{\text{body}}}

Body impedance is not constant. IEC 60479 describes how it varies with voltage, current path, contact area and skin condition, and how the effect depends on current and duration. Without quoting its curves, tens of milliamperes through the chest can be dangerous, which is why a 30 mA residual current device (RCD) is the common choice for personal protection. We use a round Rbody=1kΩR_{\text{body}} = 1\,\mathrm{k\Omega} for illustration only.

The fault loop: will the breaker trip fast enough?

Let U0U_0 be the nominal line-to-earth voltage, 230 V on a single-phase Indian supply, and ZsZ_s the earth-fault loop impedance in ohms, covering the source winding, line conductor, fault and protective conductor. Ohm’s law gives the fault current.

If=U0ZsI_f = \frac{U_0}{Z_s}

Take an illustrative Zs=0.8ΩZ_s = 0.8\,\Omega, of which Rpath=0.3ΩR_{\text{path}} = 0.3\,\Omega is the protective conductor.

If=230V0.8Ω=287.5AI_f = \frac{230\,\mathrm{V}}{0.8\,\Omega} = 287.5\,\mathrm{A}
Vt≈287.5A×0.3Ω=86VV_t \approx 287.5\,\mathrm{A} \times 0.3\,\Omega = 86\,\mathrm{V}
Ibody=86V1000Ω=86mAI_{\text{body}} = \frac{86\,\mathrm{V}}{1000\,\Omega} = 86\,\mathrm{mA}

That body current is far above the tens-of-milliamperes region, so speed protects the person. A 16 A Type B breaker trips magnetically at typically three to five times its rating, or 48 A to 80 A, so 287.5 A opens it almost at once. Standards set maximum disconnection times, so check IS 732 and the current edition.

TN and TT earthing systems

In a TN system the source neutral is earthed at the transformer and exposed metal is bonded back to it, so ZsZ_s is small. In a TT system the installation has its own electrode RAR_A and the fault returns through soil to the source electrode RBR_B, with conductor resistance RcR_c, all in ohms.

If=U0RA+RB+RcI_f = \frac{U_0}{R_A + R_B + R_c}

With illustrative values of 10, 2 and 0.5 Ω, If=230/12.5=18.4AI_f = 230/12.5 = 18.4\,\mathrm{A}. The frame sits at 18.4×10=184V18.4 \times 10 = 184\,\mathrm{V}, yet the 16 A breaker needs 48 A to 80 A and may never trip. TT systems therefore rely on an RCD of rated residual current IΔnI_{\Delta n}. A common condition follows, where 50 V is a conventional AC touch-voltage limit to confirm in the current edition.

RAIΔn≤50VR_A \, I_{\Delta n} \le 50\,\mathrm{V}

A 30 mA RCD allows RAR_A up to about 1,667Ω1{,}667\,\Omega, and a 300 mA RCD about 167Ω167\,\Omega. Ask which system your site uses.

A PV string is not a 230 V grid

A module is close to a current source. For an illustrative module with Isc=11.0AI_{sc} = 11.0\,\mathrm{A} and Imp=10.4AI_{mp} = 10.4\,\mathrm{A}, the short-circuit current is only 1.06 times the operating current. A DC earth fault cannot deliver hundreds of amperes, and fuses rated above IscI_{sc} will not see it. DC-side safety therefore leans on bonding and insulation monitoring, covered below.

Earth electrode resistance from first principles

An electrode’s resistance is not in the metal. It is in the soil around it. Start with the shape that can be solved exactly.

The hemispherical electrode

  • ρ\rho is the soil resistivity in ohm-metres (Ω·m), and II is the current leaving the electrode in amperes.
  • r0r_0 is the electrode radius and rr the distance from its centre, in metres.
  • JJ is current density in A/m², EE is electric field in V/m, and V(r)V(r) is potential in volts relative to remote earth.

Assume uniform soil, a hemisphere with its flat face at the surface, current spreading evenly and radially, steady current and remote earth at 0 V. At distance rr the current crosses a hemispherical shell of area 2πr22\pi r^2, and Ohm’s law in local form gives the field.

J=I2πr2J = \frac{I}{2\pi r^2}
E=ρJ=ρI2πr2E = \rho J = \frac{\rho I}{2\pi r^2}

The potential is the integral of the field from rr out to infinity.

V(r)=∫r∞ρI2πr′2dr′=ρI2πrV(r) = \int_r^{\infty} \frac{\rho I}{2\pi r'^2}\,dr' = \frac{\rho I}{2\pi r}

At the surface r=r0r = r_0 this is the ground potential rise. Dividing by the current gives the electrode resistance.

R=V(r0)I=ρ2πr0R = \frac{V(r_0)}{I} = \frac{\rho}{2\pi r_0}

For ρ=100Ω⋅m\rho = 100\,\Omega\cdot\mathrm{m} and r0=0.5mr_0 = 0.5\,\mathrm{m}, R=100/(2π×0.5)=31.8ΩR = 100/(2\pi \times 0.5) = 31.8\,\Omega, and at I=10AI = 10\,\mathrm{A} the electrode rises to 318 V. The soil between r0r_0 and rr holds the fraction 1−r0/r1 - r_0/r of the resistance, so at r=10r0r = 10 r_0 already 90% is counted. Nearly all the resistance sits within a few electrode radii, the electrode’s “resistance area”.

Step and touch voltage from the same field

Let ss be a stride and r1r_1 the nearer foot’s distance from the electrode centre. The step voltage is the potential difference between two points on the surface.

Vstep=V(r1)−V(r1+s)=ρI2π(1r1−1r1+s)V_{\text{step}} = V(r_1) - V(r_1 + s) = \frac{\rho I}{2\pi}\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_1 + s}\right)

With the same soil and current, r1=1mr_1 = 1\,\mathrm{m} and an illustrative stride s=0.8ms = 0.8\,\mathrm{m}, Vstep=159.2×(1−1/1.8)=70.7VV_{\text{step}} = 159.2 \times (1 - 1/1.8) = 70.7\,\mathrm{V}. Someone touching a frame bonded to the electrode, with feet at 1 m, has 318−159=159V318 - 159 = 159\,\mathrm{V} across them. Such voltages from only 10 A are why electrodes belong to a bonded, equipotential system.

Stay clear in storms: The same steep gradient surrounds a lightning down-conductor and its electrode, with currents thousands of times larger. Do not stand near the earthing of a plant, or touch its structure, during a thunderstorm.

The driven rod

A real electrode is usually a vertical rod. Sum the point-source contributions along its length, include the mirror image above the surface and average the potential on the rod. The closed result, in the form usually credited to Dwight, uses rod length LL and diameter dd, both in metres.

R=ρ2πL[ln⁡(8Ld)−1]R = \frac{\rho}{2\pi L}\left[\ln\!\left(\frac{8L}{d}\right) - 1\right]

It assumes uniform soil, a vertical rod driven flush with the surface and L≫dL \gg d. IS 3043 and other handbooks give a closely related logarithmic form, often written with ln⁡(4L/d)\ln(4L/d), so use the form in the edition you work to. We computed both for a 3 m rod of 16 mm diameter with python3.

Measuring soil resistivity and using several rods

The Wenner four-pin method

Four small pins are driven in a line at equal spacing aa in metres. A tester passes current II through the outer pair and reads the voltage ΔV\Delta V across the inner pair, giving R=ΔV/IR = \Delta V/I. It works through the hemisphere result and superposition. The first outer pin is a hemispherical source and the last an equal sink. The first inner pin is aa from the source and 2a2a from the sink, and the second inner pin is its mirror image.

VP1=ρI2π(1a−12a)=ρI4πa,VP2=−ρI4πaV_{P1} = \frac{\rho I}{2\pi}\left(\frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{2a}\right) = \frac{\rho I}{4\pi a}, \qquad V_{P2} = -\frac{\rho I}{4\pi a}
ΔV=ρI2πa⇒ρ=2πaR\Delta V = \frac{\rho I}{2\pi a} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \rho = 2\pi a R

This holds for uniform soil and pin depth much smaller than aa. In layered soil it gives an apparent resistivity, and a spacing aa senses soil to a depth of roughly the order of aa. Separate current and voltage pins also keep the current-pin contact resistance out of the voltage reading. The first row below checks directly: 2π×1×15.9=99.9Ω⋅m2\pi \times 1 \times 15.9 = 99.9\,\Omega\cdot\mathrm{m}.

Rods in parallel

Ideal parallel resistors give R/nR/n for nn rods, but rods in soil are not independent. Each raises the potential of the soil around it, and a neighbouring rod sits inside that raised region, so it collects less current. Their resistance areas overlap. For two identical rods each carrying I/2I/2, let R1R_1 be the single-rod resistance and R12R_{12} the mutual resistance, the extra voltage one rod causes at the other per ampere.

R2=R1+R122R_2 = \frac{R_1 + R_{12}}{2}

Far apart, R12→0R_{12} \to 0 and R2→R1/2R_2 \to R_1/2. Coincident, R12=R1R_{12} = R_1 and nothing is gained. A far-field estimate ρ/(2πs)\rho/(2\pi s) gives 5.3 Ω at s=3ms = 3\,\mathrm{m} and is crude there. Our own numerical line-source model reproduces the 33.5 Ω single rod and gives R12=4.4ΩR_{12} = 4.4\,\Omega, so R2=(33.5+4.4)/2=18.9ΩR_2 = (33.5 + 4.4)/2 = 18.9\,\Omega.

Earthing and bonding practice on a PV plant

Equipotential bonding

Every exposed metal part a person could touch together, such as frames, rails, trays and the inverter enclosure, should stay at nearly the same potential during a fault. Two bonded parts joined by resistance RbR_b carrying fault current II differ by IRbI R_b volts, so every joint matters. Conductors and clamps must follow the standards, notably IEC 60364-7-712, and the module maker’s manual. Copper resistance follows the R=ρl/AR = \rho\,l/A relation in our guide to solar wiring from first principles, but fault-current sizing comes from the standards.

Separate or interconnected electrodes

A plant may have an AC earth, an equipment earth and a lightning earth. Suppose 10 kA enters a lightning electrode of an illustrative 10Ω10\,\Omega. By Ohm’s law it rises to 10,000×10=100kV10{,}000 \times 10 = 100\,\mathrm{kV} above remote earth. If the inverter’s earth is a separate electrode near 0 V, all 100 kV appears across any wire joining the two systems, and across the equipment between them.

That is the physical case for bonding electrodes together, so the system rises and falls as one. Practice varies. Some plants use one interconnected grid, others keep separate lightning pits bonded directly or through an SPD. Follow IS 3043, IEC 62305 and the project design.

Insulation resistance monitoring

Most arrays are floating, with neither pole earthed. A first insulation fault then draws almost no current, which is safe, yet it leaves the array one fault from a short circuit through earth. Transformerless inverters have no galvanic isolation, so they measure the array’s insulation resistance RisoR_{iso} to earth before connecting, and often during operation. With VV the test voltage in volts and IleakI_{\text{leak}} the leakage current in amperes:

Riso=VIleakR_{iso} = \frac{V}{I_{\text{leak}}}

Leakage paths of NN strings are in parallel, so their conductances add and the total is below any single path.

1Riso=∑k=1N1Rk\frac{1}{R_{iso}} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \frac{1}{R_k}

Take 20 strings, each leaking through an illustrative 10 MΩ. The total is 10/20=0.5MΩ10/20 = 0.5\,\mathrm{M\Omega}. Now let one flooded connector drop one string to 100 kΩ.

Riso=(1910MΩ+1100kΩ)−1≈84kΩR_{iso} = \left(\frac{19}{10\,\mathrm{M\Omega}} + \frac{1}{100\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}\right)^{-1} \approx 84\,\mathrm{k\Omega}

At 600 V that leaks 600/84,000≈7.1mA600/84{,}000 \approx 7.1\,\mathrm{mA}. If the datasheet threshold is, for illustration, 100 kΩ, the inverter will not start although most of the array is healthy. Typical causes are damp connectors, dew, a nicked cable on a metal tray or a damaged backsheet. Capacitive leakage also matters on transformerless designs, one reason the switching in how a solar inverter makes AC is chosen with care.

A recurring insulation alarm is a safety signal. Do not reset it repeatedly or probe the array. A qualified electrician should find the string, and checks in our solar panel maintenance guide can catch damp connectors early.

Lightning and the voltage induced in wiring loops

Two impulse waveforms

Standard current shapes are defined in IEC 62305 and the SPD standards. A label such as 10/350 µs gives the front time, roughly how fast the current reaches its peak, then the time to fall to half of peak. The 10/350 µs impulse represents the partial current of a direct strike. Integrating a double-exponential shape with those times, we find a charge near 49 C for a 100 kA peak. The 8/20 µs impulse represents induced surges and carries of the order of twenty times less charge.

Induced voltage from the magnetic field

A nearby strike need not touch your array. Its changing field through any wiring loop induces a voltage. The notation follows.

  • μ0=4π×10−7H/m\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{H/m} is the permeability of free space, II the strike current in amperes and BB the flux density in tesla.
  • hh is the loop height parallel to the channel in metres. The loop’s near and far edges are at d1d_1 and d2d_2 from it, and AA is its area in m².
  • Φ\Phi is flux in webers, MM is mutual inductance in henries and VV is the induced voltage in volts.

Treat the channel as a long straight conductor and the loop as a rectangle in a plane containing it, the strongest coupling. Ampere’s law gives the field at distance dd.

B=μ0I2πdB = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi d}

Flux through a strip of height hh and width drdr is BhdrB\,h\,dr, so integrating across the loop gives the total.

Φ=∫d1d2μ0I2πrhdr=μ0h2πln⁡(d2d1)I\Phi = \int_{d_1}^{d_2} \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\,h\,dr = \frac{\mu_0 h}{2\pi}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{d_2}{d_1}\right) I

Flux per ampere is MM, and Faraday’s law gives the open-circuit voltage. The narrow-loop form follows from ln⁡(1+x)≈x\ln(1+x) \approx x.

V=MdIdt,M=μ0h2πln⁡(d2d1)≈μ0A2πd1V = M\,\frac{dI}{dt}, \qquad M = \frac{\mu_0 h}{2\pi}\ln\!\left(\frac{d_2}{d_1}\right) \approx \frac{\mu_0 A}{2\pi d_1}

Take an illustrative 30 kA strike rising in 1 µs, so dI/dt=3×1010A/sdI/dt = 3\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{A/s}, at d1=50md_1 = 50\,\mathrm{m} from a loop 2 m high and 5 m wide, so d2=55md_2 = 55\,\mathrm{m} and A=10m2A = 10\,\mathrm{m^2}.

M=2×10−7×2×ln⁡(1.1)=3.81×10−8HM = 2\times10^{-7} \times 2 \times \ln(1.1) = 3.81\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{H}
V=3.81×10−8×3×1010≈1,144VV = 3.81\times10^{-8} \times 3\times10^{10} \approx 1{,}144\,\mathrm{V}

The peak field at 50 m is 120 µT, and the narrow-loop formula gives 1,200 V, about 5% high. Now a loop of 0.5 m by 0.2 m, which is 0.1 m², at the same distance.

M=10−7×0.5×ln⁡(50.250)=3.99×10−10H⇒V≈12VM = 10^{-7} \times 0.5 \times \ln\!\left(\frac{50.2}{50}\right) = 3.99\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{H} \quad\Rightarrow\quad V \approx 12\,\mathrm{V}

One hundred times less area gives about one hundred times less voltage (a 1 m² loop gives about 120 V). The practical lesson is to run the positive and negative string conductors together along the same route, and to keep any bonding conductor beside them. A string whose two cables run on opposite sides of a roof encloses a large area, and the surge finds it.

Treat these as orders of magnitude. A 1 µs rise is deliberately steep: the 10/350 µs shape peaks near 7.7 kA/µs for 30 kA by our fit, inducing about 290 V in the 10 m² loop, though subsequent strokes can be faster. The model ignores screening by metal structures, and the 0.17 µs propagation delay over 50 m is not negligible. The robust result is proportionality to area.

Surge protective devices and why lead length matters

A surge protective device (SPD) is nearly an open circuit in normal use and conducts when voltage rises. Type 1 devices are tested with the 10/350 µs current, for places that may carry part of a direct strike, and Type 2 with 8/20 µs, for induced and switching surges. IEC 61643-31 covers SPDs on the DC side of PV systems and IEC 61643-32 their selection and application. Use only SPDs rated for PV DC, since a DC arc has no natural zero crossing. Their rated voltage must also exceed the array’s cold-morning open-circuit voltage, as in our string sizing guide.

  • UpU_p is the voltage protection level in volts, the highest voltage across the SPD while it conducts its rated current. It is on the datasheet.
  • UwU_w is the impulse withstand voltage of the protected equipment, in volts, also on its datasheet.
  • LL is the lead inductance in henries and dI/dtdI/dt is the surge current’s rate of change in A/s.

The equipment sees the SPD’s drop plus the inductive drop in the leads. Adding the peaks is conservative. Before any safety margin, the sum must stay below UwU_w.

Uequip≈Up+LdIdt<UwU_{\text{equip}} \approx U_p + L\,\frac{dI}{dt} \;\lt\; U_w

A straight wire has about 1 µH per metre, nearly independent of diameter because the dependence is logarithmic, so a thicker wire does not rescue you. For 1 m of lead and 10 kA/µs, which is 1010A/s10^{10}\,\mathrm{A/s}:

LdIdt=10−6H×1010A/s=10,000V=10kVL\,\frac{dI}{dt} = 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{H} \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{A/s} = 10{,}000\,\mathrm{V} = 10\,\mathrm{kV}

That is the “keep leads short” rule. A 10 kA, 8/20 µs surge rises more gently, at about 1.25 kA/µs along its front, so the same metre adds 1.25 kV. Air terminals and the rolling sphere

IEC 62305 places air terminals with the rolling-sphere method. Imagine a sphere of radius RR rolled over the structure. Wherever it can touch, a strike could attach, so terminals go there. Wherever it cannot touch, the volume is protected. The radius represents the striking distance and depends on the protection class. IEC 62305 uses classes I to IV with radii of 20, 30, 45 and 60 m.

Let hh be the mast height above the reference plane (ground or roof), with h≤Rh \le R, and rr the horizontal distance at which a sphere resting on the plane just touches the mast tip. The sphere’s centre is at height RR and horizontal distance rr, and its distance to the tip at height hh must equal RR. Pythagoras gives the result directly.

r2+(R−h)2=R2r^2 + (R - h)^2 = R^2
r=2Rh−h2r = \sqrt{2Rh - h^2}

Check it geometrically. If h=Rh = R, then r=Rr = R: the centre sits level with the tip, one radius away. As h→0h \to 0, r→0r \to 0. For h>Rh \gt R the sphere touches the mast’s side and r=Rr = R. Every point closer than rr is out of the sphere’s reach, so it is protected. The protected region is a curved surface, not a 45° cone.

As a worked check, a 3 m mast with R=45mR = 45\,\mathrm{m} gives r=2×45×3−9=261=16.2mr = \sqrt{2\times45\times3 - 9} = \sqrt{261} = 16.2\,\mathrm{m}. The shortcut 2Rh=16.4m\sqrt{2Rh} = 16.4\,\mathrm{m} is only 1.7% higher. A smaller sphere demands more terminals.

For a PV array, treat the module plane as the reference plane. Real roofs have edges and parapets that the sphere also touches, so a full design checks them all. The standards also require a separation distance between the lightning system and PV conductors, and a terminal must not shade modules. Where separation cannot be kept, parts are bonded and Type 1 SPDs may be needed. Whether a plant needs lightning protection at all is a risk-assessment question under IEC 62305.

Ask for the evidence on your own plant: the soil survey, measured electrode resistances, bonding records, SPD datasheets with lead lengths, and the commissioning insulation record. Frequently asked questions

Why does a floating DC array still need earthing?

Module frames are exposed metal, and a first insulation fault to them leaves the array one fault from a real short circuit. Bonding the frames to earth limits touch voltage and lets the inverter detect the fault by measuring insulation resistance. A qualified electrician must do this to the standards.

What does an insulation resistance fault on my inverter mean?

The inverter measured less resistance between the array and earth than its threshold allows. Common causes are damp connectors, dew, a damaged cable or a damaged module. Leakage paths from many strings add in parallel, so one poor string can pull the total down. Do not touch the array. Ask a qualified electrician to locate the string.

Does a lightning rod on the roof protect the solar panels?

It can intercept a direct strike, but the design decides how well. The rolling-sphere method sets where terminals are needed, a separation distance keeps strike current out of the PV wiring, and surge protective devices handle voltages arriving on cables. A risk assessment under IEC 62305 should drive the decision.

Why must surge protector leads be short?

Every metre of lead has roughly 1 microhenry of inductance, and the voltage across it is inductance times the rate of change of current. At 10 kA per microsecond, one metre adds about 10 kV on top of the protector’s own protection level, which can exceed what the equipment withstands.

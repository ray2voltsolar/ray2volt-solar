From Sunlight to Units: The Mathematics of Solar Energy Yield and Performance Ratio

Follow sunlight from watts per square metre to units on your meter, then see how temperature, soiling, wiring and ageing shape the performance ratio.

Four yield facts to hold on to

  • Irradiance is power per area in W/m². Irradiation is its time integral in kWh/m². A day with 5.5 kWh/m² equals 5.5 peak sun hours at 1 kW/m².
  • Ideal energy is E = P × H ÷ G, with P in kWp, H in kWh/m² and G = 1 kW/m². The performance ratio PR = actual ÷ ideal collects every loss into one number.
  • Losses multiply, so PR is a product of (1 − loss) terms. In our illustrative chain the losses add up to 22.5 per cent, but the product gives a PR of 0.794, not 0.775.
  • The site rule of 1,400 to 1,600 units per kW per year equals a capacity utilisation factor of about 16 to 18 per cent.

A quotation says your roof will make so many units a year. Behind that sentence sits a short chain of physics: sunlight reaching the panels, the panels turning it into DC at a given temperature, and the DC surviving wiring, the inverter and dust. Each link is an equation, and once you can write it you can question it.

This article builds that chain from first principles: irradiance and irradiation, plane-of-array transposition, the performance ratio of IEC 61724, temperature loss, the 4 to 4.5 units per kW per day rule of thumb we use in Andhra Pradesh, 25 years of degradation, and a 10 kWp worked example.

All site data here are illustrative, and every result is an estimate that a site audit would refine. Companion articles cover sun path, tilt angle and shading geometry and the module I-V curve, which explains the temperature coefficient.

From power density to energy: irradiance and peak sun hours

Two quantities are easily confused: power and energy. We define both before using either.

  • GG: irradiance, the solar power arriving per unit area, in W/m². It changes from second to second.
  • HH: irradiation, the solar energy arriving per unit area over a period, in kWh/m².
  • tt: time, in hours.
  • GrefG_{\mathrm{ref}}: the reference irradiance of 1 kW/m² (1,000 W/m²), also the standard test condition value.

Energy is the time integral of power, so irradiation is the time integral of irradiance over the period of interest, usually a day, a month or a year.

H=∫t1t2G(t)dtH = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} G(t)\,dt

If G is in kW/m² and t in hours, H comes out in kWh/m². A pyranometer or reference cell measures G, and a data logger approximates the integral by summing short samples.

Peak sun hours

Peak sun hours (PSH) are a unit conversion, not a count of sunshine. Define the equivalent time teqt_{\mathrm{eq}} as the hours at the reference irradiance that would deliver the same irradiation:

H=Grefteq⇒teq=HGrefH = G_{\mathrm{ref}}\,t_{\mathrm{eq}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad t_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{H}{G_{\mathrm{ref}}}

With Gref=1kW/m2G_{\mathrm{ref}} = 1\,\mathrm{kW/m^2}, teqt_{\mathrm{eq}} in hours equals H in kWh/m². That is the whole identity: a day with H=5.5kWh/m2H = 5.5\,\mathrm{kWh/m^2} has 5.5 peak sun hours. Sanity check: the sun is up for roughly twelve hours, but irradiance averages far below 1 kW/m² over them, so 5 to 6 equivalent hours is plausible.

GHI, DNI and DHI

Sunlight arrives either straight from the solar disc or scattered by the atmosphere. Databases report three components, each on a defined surface.

  • DNI\mathrm{DNI}: direct normal irradiance, in W/m². Beam light measured on a surface kept perpendicular to the sun's rays.
  • DHI\mathrm{DHI}: diffuse horizontal irradiance, in W/m². Scattered sky light falling on a horizontal surface.
  • GHI\mathrm{GHI}: global horizontal irradiance, in W/m². Everything arriving on a horizontal surface.
  • θz\theta_z: solar zenith angle, in degrees. The angle between the sun and the vertical.

A horizontal surface sees the beam at an incidence angle equal to the zenith angle, so the beam contributes DNI × cos θz to it. Adding the diffuse part gives the closure relation:

GHI=DNIcos⁡θz+DHI\mathrm{GHI} = \mathrm{DNI}\cos\theta_z + \mathrm{DHI}

Worked check, with illustrative numbers. At θz=25∘\theta_z = 25^\circ, take DNI = 700 W/m² and DHI = 130 W/m². Then GHI=700×0.9063+130=764.4W/m2\mathrm{GHI} = 700\times 0.9063 + 130 = 764.4\,\mathrm{W/m^2}, below the 1,000 W/m² reference, which is reasonable for a hazy or non-peak hour. If a source gives two components, closure recovers the third.

Transposing sunlight to the plane of the array

Panels are tilted, so the number that matters is the plane-of-array irradiance GpoaG_{\mathrm{poa}}. Databases usually give GHI, so a model must convert it. The simplest is the isotropic sky model of Liu and Jordan. Anisotropic models such as Hay-Davies and Perez add circumsolar and horizon brightening and are used in professional simulation software. At moderate tilts they differ by a few per cent of annual irradiation, and more at steep tilts.

  • β\beta: module tilt from the horizontal, in degrees.
  • θ\theta: angle of incidence between the sun's rays and the module normal, in degrees.
  • γs, γp\gamma_s,\ \gamma_p: solar azimuth and module azimuth, in degrees.
  • ρg\rho_g: ground albedo, the dimensionless fraction of GHI that the ground reflects. Values around 0.2 are commonly assumed for ordinary ground and 0.3 or more for a bright, whitewashed roof.

The beam term needs the incidence angle, from the sun-path geometry of the companion article:

cos⁡θ=cos⁡θzcos⁡β+sin⁡θzsin⁡βcos⁡(γs−γp)\cos\theta = \cos\theta_z\cos\beta + \sin\theta_z\sin\beta\cos(\gamma_s-\gamma_p)

When the sun is behind the module plane, cos θ is negative and the beam term is set to zero.

Deriving the sky view factor

Assume uniform sky radiance LL in W/m²/sr over the upper hemisphere, which is what isotropic means. A surface with unit normal n^\hat n receives ∫L(n^⋅s^)dΩ\int L\,(\hat n\cdot\hat s)\,d\Omega from the directions s^\hat s it can see, with dΩd\Omega the solid angle in steradians. For a horizontal surface:

DHI=L∫02π∫0π/2cos⁡αsin⁡αdαdφ=πL\mathrm{DHI} = L\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\alpha\,\sin\alpha\,d\alpha\,d\varphi = \pi L

Here α\alpha is the polar angle from the vertical and φ\varphi the azimuth. Now tilt the surface by β. The module's front hemisphere (where n^⋅s^>0\hat n\cdot\hat s\gt 0) splits into a sky part and a ground part, with contributions per unit radiance EskyE_{\mathrm{sky}} and EgndE_{\mathrm{gnd}}. Together they fill the front hemisphere:

Esky+Egnd=πE_{\mathrm{sky}} + E_{\mathrm{gnd}} = \pi

For a second relation, integrate n^⋅s^\hat n\cdot\hat s over the whole upper hemisphere with no visibility cut. Horizontal components cancel by symmetry and the vertical one integrates to π, so ∫s^dΩ=πz^\int \hat s\,d\Omega = \pi\hat z and the result is πcos⁡β\pi\cos\beta. This counts the visible sky positively and the sky behind the module plane negatively. Reflecting that region through the origin maps it onto the ground region in the module's front hemisphere, so its magnitude is EgndE_{\mathrm{gnd}}. Therefore

Esky−Egnd=πcos⁡βE_{\mathrm{sky}} - E_{\mathrm{gnd}} = \pi\cos\beta

Adding and subtracting the two relations gives the sky view factor and the ground view factor:

Esky=π1+cos⁡β2,Egnd=π1−cos⁡β2E_{\mathrm{sky}} = \pi\,\frac{1+\cos\beta}{2}, \qquad E_{\mathrm{gnd}} = \pi\,\frac{1-\cos\beta}{2}

Dividing by π\pi (since DHI=πL\mathrm{DHI}=\pi L), the module receives the fraction (1+cos⁡β)/2(1+\cos\beta)/2 of the diffuse irradiance. Sanity check: at β=0\beta = 0 it is 1, and at β=90∘\beta = 90^\circ it is one half, as for a vertical wall. A uniform diffuse ground of radiance ρgGHI/π\rho_g\,\mathrm{GHI}/\pi gives, by the same argument, the ground-reflected term GHIρg(1−cos⁡β)/2\mathrm{GHI}\,\rho_g\,(1-\cos\beta)/2. The assumptions are a flat unobstructed horizon, a uniform sky and uniform ground. Nearby walls, parapets and trees break all three, which is why shade surveys matter.

The Liu and Jordan result

Gpoa=DNIcos⁡θ+DHI1+cos⁡β2+GHIρg1−cos⁡β2G_{\mathrm{poa}} = \mathrm{DNI}\cos\theta + \mathrm{DHI}\,\frac{1+\cos\beta}{2} + \mathrm{GHI}\,\rho_g\,\frac{1-\cos\beta}{2}

The three terms are beam, sky diffuse and ground-reflected. Worked example, illustrative: sun at θz=25∘\theta_z = 25^\circ, tilt β=15∘\beta = 15^\circ facing the sun's azimuth so θ=10∘\theta = 10^\circ, DNI = 700, DHI = 130, GHI = 764.4 W/m² and ρg=0.2\rho_g = 0.2.

Ideal energy and the performance ratio

Module ratings are given at standard test conditions (STC): 1,000 W/m², 25 °C cell temperature and a defined air-mass spectrum. The nameplate kWp is the DC power under those conditions. We ask what energy an ideal plant would deliver, then compare the real plant with it.

  • PSTCP_{\mathrm{STC}}: array rating at STC, in kWp.
  • GSTCG_{\mathrm{STC}}: STC irradiance, 1 kW/m².
  • HpoaH_{\mathrm{poa}}: plane-of-array irradiation over the period, in kWh/m².
  • Eideal, EactualE_{\mathrm{ideal}},\ E_{\mathrm{actual}}: ideal and measured energy at the AC meter, in kWh.

Assume an ideal array whose power is proportional to irradiance, with the cell at 25 °C and no other losses. Then P(t)=PSTCGpoa(t)/GSTCP(t) = P_{\mathrm{STC}}\,G_{\mathrm{poa}}(t)/G_{\mathrm{STC}}, and integrating gives

Eideal=PSTCGSTC∫Gpoadt=PSTCHpoaGSTCE_{\mathrm{ideal}} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{STC}}}{G_{\mathrm{STC}}}\int G_{\mathrm{poa}}\,dt = \frac{P_{\mathrm{STC}}\,H_{\mathrm{poa}}}{G_{\mathrm{STC}}}

Unit check: kWp×kWh/m2÷kW/m2=kWh\mathrm{kWp}\times\mathrm{kWh/m^2}\div\mathrm{kW/m^2} = \mathrm{kWh}. Example: 10 kWp on a day with Hpoa=5.5kWh/m2H_{\mathrm{poa}} = 5.5\,\mathrm{kWh/m^2} gives 10×5.5÷1=55kWh10\times 5.5\div 1 = 55\,\mathrm{kWh}, which is 10 kW for 5.5 peak sun hours.

The performance ratio

IEC 61724 defines the monitoring quantities for photovoltaic systems. Two of them are the reference yield Yr=Hpoa/GSTCY_r = H_{\mathrm{poa}}/G_{\mathrm{STC}} in hours and the final yield Yf=Eactual/PSTCY_f = E_{\mathrm{actual}}/P_{\mathrm{STC}} in kWh/kWp, which is also hours. Their ratio is the performance ratio:

PR=YfYr=EactualEideal=EactualGSTCPSTCHpoa\mathrm{PR} = \frac{Y_f}{Y_r} = \frac{E_{\mathrm{actual}}}{E_{\mathrm{ideal}}} = \frac{E_{\mathrm{actual}}\,G_{\mathrm{STC}}}{P_{\mathrm{STC}}\,H_{\mathrm{poa}}}

PR is dimensionless and not an efficiency: it compares output with what the nameplate rating would deliver, which makes plants comparable. PR falls in hot weather because the reference stays at 25 °C, so compare like periods with like. The denominator also needs plane-of-array data from a pyranometer or reference cell, since a satellite-based PR inherits the satellite error.

The multiplicative loss chain

Energy flows through a series of stages: the module, the DC wiring, the inverter and the AC wiring to the meter. Each stage passes on a fraction of what it receives. Let lil_i be the fractional loss at stage i, a dimensionless number between 0 and 1. Stage i retains 1−li1-l_i, so after n stages

PR=∏i=1n(1−li)\mathrm{PR} = \prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-l_i)

This is exact when the stages act in series on the same energy stream, each loss defined against the energy entering that stage. Simulation tools split losses differently, so treat it as a close approximation. Expanding for small lil_i shows what simply adding percentages loses:

∏i(1−li)=1−∑ili+∑i<jlilj−∑i<j<kliljlk+⋯\prod_{i}(1-l_i) = 1 - \sum_i l_i + \sum_{i\lt j} l_i\,l_j - \sum_{i\lt j\lt k} l_i\,l_j\,l_k + \cdots

The first-order estimate 1−∑li1-\sum l_i is slightly low because the pair term is positive: a second loss acts on energy already reduced, so it removes a little less than its face value.

Temperature loss and why it must be irradiance-weighted

Silicon cells lose voltage as they warm, so output falls with temperature. In India this is usually the largest single loss, so it gets its own derivation. Notation first.

  • TcT_c: cell temperature, in °C.
  • TaT_a: ambient air temperature, in °C.
  • γ\gamma: power temperature coefficient, in 1/K. It is negative for silicon, and a typical datasheet value is about −0.0030K−1-0.0030\,\mathrm{K^{-1}} (that is, −0.30 % per kelvin). Use your own module datasheet.
  • NOCT\mathrm{NOCT}: nominal operating cell temperature from the datasheet, in °C. We use 45 °C as an illustrative value.
  • kk: heating coefficient, in K·m²/W.

Assume the cell runs above ambient in proportion to irradiance. NOCT is defined at 800 W/m² and 20 °C ambient, so the rise there is NOCT − 20 and

Tc=Ta+kG,k=NOCT−20800T_c = T_a + kG, \qquad k = \frac{\mathrm{NOCT}-20}{800}

For NOCT = 45 °C, k=25/800=0.03125Km2/Wk = 25/800 = 0.03125\,\mathrm{K\,m^2/W}, so a cell at 1,000 W/m² runs about 31 K above ambient. This ignores wind and mounting. Close-mounted modules on a concrete roof run hotter than the datasheet suggests, perhaps by several kelvin. To first order, the power at irradiance G and cell temperature TcT_c is

P=PSTCGGSTC[1+γ(Tc−25)]P = P_{\mathrm{STC}}\,\frac{G}{G_{\mathrm{STC}}}\left[1+\gamma\,(T_c-25)\right]

Integrating over the period, the energy is E=(PSTC/GSTC)∫G[1+γ(Tc−25)]dtE = (P_{\mathrm{STC}}/G_{\mathrm{STC}})\int G\,[1+\gamma(T_c-25)]\,dt. Pull the constants out and define the irradiance-weighted mean cell temperature:

T‾w=∫GTcdt∫Gdt,E=PSTCHGSTC[1+γ(T‾w−25)]\bar T_w = \frac{\int G\,T_c\,dt}{\int G\,dt}, \qquad E = \frac{P_{\mathrm{STC}}H}{G_{\mathrm{STC}}}\left[1+\gamma(\bar T_w-25)\right]

The temperature loss is exactly lT=−γ(T‾w−25)l_T = -\gamma(\bar T_w - 25), and it uses T‾w\bar T_w, not the simple time average. Energy is the quantity being summed, and it arrives mostly when the sun is high. Cool early-morning and late-afternoon hours contribute little energy, so averaging them in equally would flatter the plant.

Specific yield, CUF and the site rule of thumb

Two numbers compare plants of different sizes: specific yield, met above as final yield, and the capacity utilisation factor, which compares energy with what the array would make at full rated power for every hour of the year.

  • YfY_f: specific (final) yield, in kWh/kWp, equal numerically to hours.
  • CUF\mathrm{CUF}: capacity utilisation factor, dimensionless, often quoted in per cent.
  • 8760h8760\,\mathrm{h}: hours in a non-leap year.
Yf=EPSTC,CUF=EPSTC×8760h=Yf8760hY_f = \frac{E}{P_{\mathrm{STC}}}, \qquad \mathrm{CUF} = \frac{E}{P_{\mathrm{STC}}\times 8760\,\mathrm{h}} = \frac{Y_f}{8760\,\mathrm{h}}

Some reports use AC capacity in the denominator, so check which one a quoted CUF uses. Combining the PR definition with the reference yield gives a forecast formula, Yf=PR×YrY_f = \mathrm{PR}\times Y_r, where YrY_r is numerically the annual HpoaH_{\mathrm{poa}} in kWh/m².

Reconciling with 4 to 4.5 units per kW per day

Our working rule for this part of Andhra Pradesh is about 4 to 4.5 units per kW per day, or roughly 1,400 to 1,600 units per kW per year. A unit is one kWh. Convert it:

CUF=14008760=16.0%,CUF=16008760=18.3%\mathrm{CUF} = \frac{1400}{8760} = 16.0\%, \qquad \mathrm{CUF} = \frac{1600}{8760} = 18.3\%

Dividing the annual range by 365 gives 3.8 to 4.4 units per kW per day, matching the quoted daily figures within the rounding that "about" allows. Now work backwards to the implied PR. For an illustrative plane-of-array irradiation of 5.3 to 5.6 kWh/m² per day, the annual YrY_r is 1,934.5 to 2,044 hours, and PR=Yf/Yr\mathrm{PR} = Y_f/Y_r.

Degradation and seasonal variation

Degradation over a lifetime

Modules lose a small fraction of output each year. Let d be the annual degradation rate (dimensionless, per year), E1E_1 the first-year energy in kWh and EnE_n the energy in year n. Assume a constant rate and the same weather every year. Each year retains 1−d1-d of the previous one, so

En=E1(1−d)n−1E_n = E_1\,(1-d)^{\,n-1}

The lifetime energy over N years is a geometric series with ratio 1−d1-d:

SN=E1∑n=1N(1−d)n−1=E11−(1−d)NdS_N = E_1\sum_{n=1}^{N}(1-d)^{n-1} = E_1\,\frac{1-(1-d)^N}{d}

For small d the binomial expansion (1−d)n−1≈1−(n−1)d(1-d)^{n-1}\approx 1-(n-1)d gives a linear approximation. Summing it with ∑n=1N(n−1)=N(N−1)/2\sum_{n=1}^{N}(n-1) = N(N-1)/2:

SNlin=E1[N−dN(N−1)2]S_N^{\mathrm{lin}} = E_1\left[N - d\,\frac{N(N-1)}{2}\right]

Worked example. Take an illustrative d=0.005d = 0.005 per year, N=25N = 25 years and the first-year energy of the 10 kWp example below, E1=15,936E_1 = 15{,}936 kWh.

Seasons and monsoon months

Monthly energy follows irradiation, modified by temperature and soiling. Clear spring months give the highest irradiation but hot cells and dusty glass, so PR dips while energy peaks. Monsoon months bring cloud, which cuts the beam and raises the diffuse share, and rain, which washes the glass. Winter has a lower sun but cooler cells and a higher PR. Sanity checks: 4.37 units per kW per day lies inside the 4 to 4.5 rule, and 1,594 kWh/kWp sits at the top of the 1,400 to 1,600 range. Over 25 years with 0.5 per cent degradation, lifetime energy is about 375,000 kWh, roughly 15,000 kWh a year.

How uncertain is this?

With every loss at the optimistic end of its table range the PR becomes 0.863 (about 17,300 kWh). At the pessimistic end it falls to 0.710 (about 14,300 kWh). Raising soiling from 3 to 6 per cent alone cuts the result by 3.1 per cent, and a temperature loss of 12 per cent instead of 9 cuts it by 3.3 per cent. The estimate is a range of a few thousand kWh, narrowed only by a site audit, a shade study and local weather data.

Where to act: After installation you influence soiling and availability, through cleaning and prompt fault response. Temperature, wiring and inverter losses are largely fixed at design stage. A falling monthly PR flags a problem before the bill does.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between irradiance and irradiation?

Irradiance is power per unit area in watts per square metre, and it changes second by second. Irradiation is the energy arriving per unit area over a period, in kilowatt-hours per square metre, and it is the time integral of irradiance. Yield calculations use irradiation because your meter records energy, not power.

What does a peak sun hour mean?

One peak sun hour is 1 kWh/m² of irradiation, the energy that falls in one hour at 1,000 W/m². A day with 5.5 kWh/m² has 5.5 peak sun hours even though the sun is up for about twelve. The idea converts a day of varying sunlight into an equivalent number of hours at full strength.

What performance ratio should a rooftop plant achieve?

Well-built plants often report a performance ratio from the high 70s to the low 80s per cent, but the figure depends on climate, soiling, design and how it is measured. Treat that as a typical range, not a guarantee. Compare your plant with its own history and a site-specific estimate, using the same period and method.

Why are summer months not always the best for output?

Hot cells lose voltage. Output falls about 0.3 to 0.4 per cent for each degree the cell is above 25 degrees Celsius, and a roof-mounted cell can run 25 to 35 degrees above ambient in strong sun. Spring still gives high energy because irradiation is high, but the performance ratio dips in the hottest, dustiest weeks.

How many units per kW can I expect in a year?

A rule of thumb for this part of Andhra Pradesh is about 4 to 4.5 units per kW per day, or roughly 1,400 to 1,600 units per kW per year. The real figure depends on tilt, shade, soiling, equipment and weather. A site audit and a simulation with measured irradiance data give a better estimate for your roof.

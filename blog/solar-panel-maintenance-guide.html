Solar Panel Maintenance: A Practical Cleaning and Upkeep Guide for India

Your panels need regular cleaning, but the timing and method matter. Use this seasonal plan to remove dust safely, check electrical parts and know when to call a technician.

Key takeaways

  • Dry-season dust, road emissions and farm particles can cut output by an estimated 5% to 20% when panels stay dirty.
  • Wash before the roof heats up or after it cools. Cold water on glass above 60°C can create damaging temperature stress.
  • Use low-mineral water with a soft microfibre brush; hard borewell water can leave scale on the glass.
  • Arrange yearly checks of earth resistance, MC4 connectors and inverter logs, and keep the maintenance record for warranty claims.

Solar panels have no motors or gears to service, but an exposed roof still collects dirt. If you leave a home or business system alone for months, the glass can lose sunlight even while the inverter appears normal. Put cleaning and inspection into your routine from the day the system is commissioned.

In Andhra Pradesh, summer dust, highway emissions, pollen, cement from nearby work and bird droppings settle on the glass. Engineers call this layer soiling. You do not need fuel or lubrication, but you do need to remove that layer before it becomes a persistent source of lost generation.

Depending on the site and weather, neglected soiling may reduce daily output by an estimated 5% to 20%. That matters on a 3 kW home roof and even more on a 10 kW commercial installation. The steps below explain when to wash, what to keep away from the glass and which electrical checks belong with a qualified technician.

How dust reduces your solar generation

A panel produces electricity when light passes through its protective glass and reaches the silicon cells. Dust on the surface blocks or scatters some of that light. A thin, even layer may look harmless from the ground, yet its effect appears in the daily unit reading, particularly after weeks without rain.

1. Loss builds up between washes

On a dry, low-tilt roof in Rayalaseema or coastal Andhra Pradesh, dust can build steadily. For planning, one site-dependent example assumes 0.2% to 0.6% daily loss, 10% to 15% after four rainless weeks and up to 25% near busy roads, crushers or rice mills. Use inverter records and the weather to set your cleaning interval; those figures are not a measured result for your roof.

For a well-sited system here, use roughly 4 to 4.5 units per kW per day as an annual average, or about 1,400 to 1,600 units per kW per year. If a 5 kW home system falls from 4.5 to 3.4 units per kW per day because of soiling, it can miss more than 150 units in a month. Your actual loss depends on shade, weather, roof angle and grid availability.

2. Bird droppings can create hot spots

A patch of bird droppings shades a small area more severely than an even film of dust. If it covers two or three cells on a 550 Wp module, those cells produce less current while nearby cells remain in sunlight. Repeated local shading can create a hot spot, so remove such deposits promptly and gently.

Under severe mismatch, a shaded cell can enter reverse bias and turn electrical energy into heat. A hot spot can reach above 140°C in the example described here, discolor the EVA and backsheet, and damage the silicon. Whether a particular mark is a hot spot needs inspection; do not diagnose it from a photograph alone. Neglect-related damage may also affect a warranty claim.

Act on local shading: Look over the array regularly for droppings. Remove them with soft water and a microfibre sponge when the panel is cool, without reaching across an unsafe roof edge. If you see browning or repeated output loss, ask a technician to check the cells and bypass diodes.

Clean the glass without risking damage or a fall

Use simple tools, but plan the work around temperature and roof access. Scratched coatings, cracked glass and falls cost far more than a missed wash. If the array is hard to reach safely, have a trained person do the cleaning.

Rule 1: Wash when the panels are cool

At peak sun, roughly 10:30 AM to 4:30 PM, panel glass can reach about 55°C to 68°C. Water at 22°C to 28°C creates a sharp temperature difference on that surface. This thermal shock may damage glass or cells, particularly if a panel already has a weakness.

Choose 6:00 AM to 8:00 AM or wait until after 5:30 PM, once the glass has cooled. Avoid washing during a hot afternoon simply because that is when the roof is convenient to access. Check that the walkway itself is dry and safe before carrying water onto the terrace.

Rule 2: Keep mineral deposits off the glass

Borewell water in parts of Andhra Pradesh can have high total dissolved solids, sometimes in the 300 to 800 ppm range. Calcium and magnesium left behind as it dries form white scale. Repeated deposits on the anti-reflective coating become difficult to remove and reduce light transmission.

Prefer soft municipal or filtered water, ideally below 100 ppm TDS. If borewell water is the only option, wash when the panels are cool and draw it off with a squeegee before it dries. Do not scrub hardened scale with a blade; ask the panel maker or installer how to remove it safely.

Use the right water: RO reject water may be useful for gardening or rinsing the mounting structure, but it can be mineral-rich. Reserve low-TDS water for the panel glass and remove standing drops before they leave marks.

Rule 3: Use a soft brush and plain water

Keep wire brushes, metal scrapers, scouring pads, laundry detergent, phenyl and acid away from the modules. Abrasives and chemicals can damage the anti-reflective layer, which may be about 100 nanometres thick. A dedicated soft-bristle solar brush or microfibre mop on a telescopic aluminium pole is enough for ordinary dust.

Rule 4: Keep your weight off the modules

A module may be tested for distributed loads of 2,400 Pa from wind and 5,400 Pa from snow. That does not mean it can take a person's footstep. Concentrating 60 kg to 85 kg on one point can flex the glass and crack the thin silicon cells underneath. Keep a clear walking route around the array instead.

A cleaning schedule for each season

Rainfall, dust and sun angle change the work your roof needs. Use this schedule as a starting point, then adjust it using your inverter readings and what you see on the panels.

Season and interval Work to plan What to inspect What to avoid
Dry summer (Feb to May)
Every 12 to 15 days		 Wash early with soft water; lift off pollen and droppings; remove debris below the array. Compare daily units in the log and make sure air reaches the inverter. Skip the 10:00 AM to 5:00 PM heat and do not wipe dusty glass with a dry abrasive cloth.
Monsoon (June to Sept)
Monthly or as needed		 Keep water draining and clear mud from the lower aluminium frame. Look for wet conduit clamps and arrange an earthing check after severe storms. Do not count on light drizzle to clean a dusty panel; it may leave a mud line.
After monsoon (Oct to Nov)
Wash every two weeks		 Remove algae and settled grime; trim branches shading the roof. Have structure bolts and module clamps checked after strong wind. Do not leave new tree shade in place as the winter sun sits lower.
Winter & smog (Dec to Jan)
Every 15 to 20 days		 Rinse off soot, road film and construction dust held by morning dew. Review DC string voltage ($V_{oc}$) on a clear, cool morning. Keep jets above 30 bar away from junction boxes and cable joints.

Check the effect: Ray2Volt field observations in Tirupati and Srikalahasti have shown an immediate 8% to 18% rise in inverter power after a 15-minute wash of dusty panels. Treat that as a site observation, not an expected gain on every roof; compare readings under similar sunlight.

Inspect the wiring, earthing and supports

Clean glass is only one part of maintenance. Solar strings can operate at 300 V to 800 V DC, so leave electrical opening and testing to a qualified technician. Schedule periodic checks of the earth system, inverter cooling and cable supports as well as the modules.

1. Test the earthing and lightning path

A typical design has separate pits for AC earthing, DC earthing and lightning protection. Dry summer soil around an electrode can raise resistance. Ask the installer how its earthing chambers should be maintained and keep them accessible for testing.

Where the installation design calls for it, add water to the earthing chamber in dry weather and aim for a measured resistance below 5 ohms. After monsoon, have an electrician use a digital earth tester to check continuity. The reading, rather than a visual glance, tells you whether the path is working.

2. Keep the inverter's cooling path open

The inverter sheds heat through aluminium fins at its rear. Spiders, dust and leaves can block airflow, especially in an outdoor utility area. About once every three months, clear accessible fins with a dry, soft brush or low-pressure air, following the maker's instructions. Record recurring messages such as "Isolation Fault" or "Grid Voltage High" and give them to a technician.

3. Look for exposed cables and loose connectors

Outdoor DC cable faces sun and rain throughout the year. At the annual service, have a technician confirm it stays secured in UV-resistant PVC conduit or metal tray and that MC4 connectors are sound. From a safe position, you can spot cable loops touching hot sheet metal or rubbing on rough concrete; report them without touching the wiring.

Choose between self-cleaning and a service contract

The safe choice depends on roof access, array height and plant size. You may clean an accessible home system yourself, while electrical tests and difficult roofs call for trained workers.

Home systems of 1 kW to 10 kW

On a flat RCC terrace with a secure parapet, you may be able to wash a home solar system from a safe walkway. A hose, 4-metre telescopic microfibre pole and rubber squeegee can cover a typical 3 kW array of five to six panels in under 20 minutes every couple of weeks. First make sure you can reach every panel without stepping on one.

For arrays on structures 8 to 10 feet high or on sloping tiles without a protected edge, bring in a trained technician with fall protection. A local annual maintenance contract (AMC) is also useful when you cannot inspect the roof regularly or need someone to track generation and service records.

Commercial and industrial plants of 20 kW to 500 kW+

A broad factory, hotel, school or warehouse roof needs a planned cleaning route and safety method. Carrying buckets across sheet roofing is rarely practical. For sites like those in our commercial solar services and manufacturing guide, an operations & maintenance (O&M) plan can include:

  • Roof water lines with outlet points roughly every 15 metres, while keeping spray pressure within panel maker limits.
  • Semi-automatic or robotic brushes where they reduce water use and avoid unsafe access.
  • An AMC with thermal camera or drone inspection to identify suspect bypass diodes and hot spots for further testing.

Keep records that support a warranty claim

Ray2Volt quotes panels with a 12-year product warranty and a 30-year performance warranty. The performance terms may specify about 84% to 87% output in year 30 for a given model; read its actual curve. Makers can exclude damage from neglect or misuse, including:

  • Cracked glass or cells after someone steps on a module.
  • Corrosion or coating damage linked to acids, abrasive pads or unapproved cleaning agents.
  • Hot spot damage left to worsen under persistent bird droppings.
  • Glass damage that follows cold-water washing on a hot afternoon.

Keep a log of cleaning dates, fault codes and monthly units, either on paper or through the inverter app. Photos before and after work can help explain a later claim. Roof safety: Stay off a wet or sloping roof unless trained workers have proper fall protection. Do not touch a damaged cable or open a junction box. Report electrical faults and ask a qualified solar technician to investigate them.

Questions about panel upkeep

How often should you wash panels in Andhra Pradesh?

Start with a wash every 15 to 20 days on a dry-season home roof in Tirupati, Srikalahasti or Puttur. A plant near a highway or factory may need a 10 to 14 day cycle. Adjust the interval by comparing output and visible dust after each wash.

Which cleaning liquids should stay off panel glass?

Avoid laundry or dish detergent, bleach, acid and abrasive cleaners. They can leave a film, damage the anti-reflective coating or affect frame seals. Plain low-mineral water and a soft brush are the safer starting point; follow the panel maker's cleaning instructions.

Does monsoon rain replace manual cleaning?

Heavy rain can rinse loose dust. Light drizzle may leave a mud line against the lower aluminium frame, and rain will not reliably remove bird droppings. Inspect the array after the season and wash remaining deposits with soft water.

Why should you wait until hot panels cool before washing?

Midday glass can reach about 55°C to 68°C. Much cooler water causes a sudden temperature change that may crack glass or cells, particularly where a module is already stressed. Wash in the early morning or after the panels cool in the evening.

What output loss is possible after two dry months without cleaning?

An illustrative dry-season estimate allows for a 12% to 22% drop after two dusty months, with losses above 25% possible beside heavy industrial fallout. Your roof may differ. Compare clear-day inverter readings before and after a wash, accounting for shade and weather.

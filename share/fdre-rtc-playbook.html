Knowledge base · Utility-scale renewables

FDRE & RTC: firm renewable power, from first principles

A solar plant sells energy. A firm renewable plant sells a promise: this many megawatts, in these hours, every day, for 25 years, whatever the weather does. India's round-the-clock (RTC) and firm-and-dispatchable (FDRE) contracts are that promise written down, with a price for keeping it and a penalty for breaking it. This page builds the idea from the grid upward, one piece at a time, and ends with working models of the physics and the money.

Why developers want these contracts

A 25-year contract with a central-government counterparty, a tariff close to double plain solar, and operating margins near 90%: once built, the plant has almost no fuel or variable cost. It behaves like a long-dated annuity, which is why pension funds, infrastructure funds and InvITs pay a premium for operating portfolios.

Where the returns come from

Mostly from leverage. The project on its own earns around – (project IRR). Borrowing 70–75% of the cost at about 9% lifts the owner's return to –. On top of that: excess energy sold on the exchanges, cheaper refinancing once the plant has a track record, and selling a stake after commissioning to a buyer who accepts a lower return.

What erodes them

Three things, in order of size. Construction cost: on this model, every 5% of capex overrun costs about – of equity IRR. Commissioning late: three months costs about –. Winter penalties, if the plant is sized too lean. The first two are decided before the plant ever runs, which is why the build phase sets most of the return.

The one idea to hold onto

Sunlight and wind arrive on their own schedule. A firm contract asks for power on the buyer's schedule. The whole business is the gap between those two schedules: how much extra plant, how much storage and how much risk it takes to close it, and what that is worth to the buyer.

Part 1 · The market

Who's who in the grid

Power passes through four hands between a plant and a light switch. Follow the chain once and the rest of the market is just these four parties making deals with each other.

Distribution company. Owns the local wires and the customer relationship in its territory. Usually state-owned, and usually loss-making, because the retail tariff it may charge farmers and households is below what the power costs. That is why its payment record matters so much to anyone selling to it, and why so much contract machinery exists to protect the seller from the buyer.

ISTS and transmission

Power Grid runs the inter-state transmission system, the high-voltage backbone that lets a plant in Rajasthan sell to a DISCOM in Tamil Nadu. Almost every large renewable project connects to it. Using it costs a monthly charge per MW, which the government waives for renewables, with conditions that matter later.

Grid-India and the 15-minute block

The national, regional and state load dispatch centres keep supply equal to demand second by second. Every plant tells them a day ahead what it will inject in each 15-minute block, and is charged for deviating. The block is the grid's unit of time; every contract test on this page is built from it.

The referees

CERC, the central regulator, governs anything inter-state: transmission charges, deviation penalties, adopting auction tariffs. State regulators (SERCs) set retail tariffs and approve what a DISCOM may buy. The Ministry of Power and MNRE write the standard bidding guidelines that every RTC and FDRE tender follows.

Part 1 · The market

How a DISCOM buys power

Think of a DISCOM as a buyer who must fill 35,040 small buckets a year, one per 15-minute block, each holding exactly that block's demand. It fills most of them with long-term contracts and tops up the rest on the exchanges, and the price of the last litre in each bucket is what matters.

Long-term PPAs

25-year contracts with generators, covering 85–90% of a DISCOM's energy. Older coal plants are paid cost-plus (Section 62 of the Electricity Act: the regulator checks the plant's costs and allows a return on top). Nearly all renewable capacity is competitively bid (Section 63: the price is discovered in an auction and the regulator simply adopts it).

Power exchanges

IEX and others run a day-ahead market (DAM) and a real-time market (RTM) with a price for every 15-minute block. DISCOMs buy their shortfalls and sell their surpluses here. Prices swing from under ₹2/kWh at noon to the ₹10/kWh ceiling on summer evenings.

Renewable purchase obligation

Each DISCOM must source a rising share of its energy from renewables (around 30% in 2025–26, climbing towards 43% by 2030), with separate sub-targets for wind, hydro and, under recent rules, energy storage. Missing it costs money. This obligation is the policy pull behind every renewable tender.

The merit order: which plants run, and what the last one costsIllustrative all-India stack of dispatchable plants, cheapest first. Solar and wind are not on it because they run whenever the resource is there, at near-zero marginal cost, which pushes the stack to the right.

Plants are switched on cheapest first, like seats filled on a bus. The last plant needed sets the price for everyone in that block. At noon, once solar has taken its share, the need is small and a cheap pithead coal plant is the last one on. At 8 pm the need is large and an expensive gas or imported-coal plant is the last one on. The difference between those two prices is the whole story of firm renewables.

What the DISCOM pays by hourIllustrative day-ahead exchange price on a summer day, against the flat tariffs of a plain solar PPA and an FDRE PPA.
Exchange pricePlain solar PPA ₹2.6FDRE PPA ₹4.5

Plain solar delivers in the cheap hours, so ₹2.6 is less of a bargain than it looks: the DISCOM could often have bought those same hours on the exchange for ₹2–3. FDRE power arrives in the hours the DISCOM would otherwise pay ₹8–10, so ₹4.5 can be the cheaper purchase. A buyer compares contracts on when the energy comes, not only on the rupees per unit.

Marginal cost

What one more unit costs once the plant exists: fuel and little else. Near zero for solar, wind, hydro and nuclear, ₹2–4 for coal, ₹6–9 for gas. The cost of building the plant is separate and is paid whether or not it runs, which is why solar at noon is free to dispatch yet still has to earn back its capex somehow.

Landed cost

What the DISCOM actually pays at its doorstep: the generator's tariff plus transmission charges, losses and any trading margin. Two contracts with the same tariff can land at different costs, and the transmission waiver is the usual reason.

Part 1 · The market

Demand: the load curve

Add up what every household, farm and factory in the country is drawing at each moment and plot it over a day. The shape that appears decides which kind of power is valuable and which is not.

MW vs MWh

Megawatts are a rate, like the width of a pipe: how much power is flowing right now. Megawatt-hours are a quantity, like water in a tank: energy over time. A 100 MW plant running for four hours delivers 400 MWh. Contracts are written in MW; bills are paid per MWh. Every firm product turns out to be a promise about MW in particular hours.

Base, peak and ramp

Base load is the demand that never goes away, the level at 3 am. The peak is the highest point of the day. A ramp is how fast demand, or the gap other plants must fill, rises or falls, in MW per hour. Steep ramps are hard for slow plants like coal to follow, which is the first hint of why storage will matter.

Two humps: a morning peak as offices and industry start, and a bigger evening peak as lighting, cooking and air-conditioning overlap. The evening one is the expensive one, because the sun has set.

The year has a shape too. Demand peaks in April–June with cooling load and again in September–October. Wind is strongest in the June–September monsoon and weakest from October to February. So the DISCOM's hardest hours are autumn and winter evenings: demand still high, no sun, little wind. Every firm-power design is really a design for those hours.
Part 1 · The market

Generation: how each source produces

A DISCOM fills its load curve by stacking sources that each produce in their own pattern. The pattern is the point: a megawatt of coal and a megawatt of solar are different products.

Capacity utilisation factor (CUF)

Energy made in a year as a share of what the plant would make running flat out for all 8,760 hours. Solar manages 20–25% on its AC rating (more with extra DC and trackers), wind 30–40% at a good site, coal 55–70%, nuclear over 80%. CUF says how much energy a MW gives; it says nothing about when. In renewable tenders it also serves as the contractual minimum a plain solar or hybrid plant must deliver.

A day in the life of each sourceOutput as a share of installed capacity on a typical day. Shaded areas are the plant's own limits.

Coal

Runs all day. Can turn down only to a technical minimum (about 55%), and ramps slowly. Base load.

Hydro (reservoir)

Stores water, so it can run hard for a few hours. Used for peaks. Limited by the season's inflow.

Solar

A bell between sunrise and sunset. Zero for 12–14 hours. Peaks when demand is middling.

Wind

Blows all day, strongest in the evening and night at Indian sites. Big swings day to day and season to season.

Dispatchable vs variable

Coal, gas, hydro and storage run when told to: dispatchable. Solar and wind run when they can: variable, treated by the grid as must-run except for grid security. A firm renewable product is an attempt to make a bundle of variable plants behave, from the buyer's side of the meter, like one dispatchable plant.

Solar's success problem

Now put the pieces together. Lay solar output on top of the load curve and look at what is left for everyone else to supply. That leftover, demand minus solar and wind, is called net load, and its shape is why DISCOMs have started paying for firm power.

What this does to the DISCOM. Each extra gigawatt of plain solar makes noon cheaper and does nothing for 8 pm. The DISCOM still has to keep coal and gas plants on contract for the evening, and pay their fixed charges while they sit idle at midday. Past a point, more plain solar is worth less and less to the buyer, and on some days part of it is switched off at noon because nobody needs it. The curve above, with its sagging middle and steep evening climb, is nicknamed the duck curve.

What a firm contract changes. RTC and FDRE move the shaping problem to the generator. The developer combines solar, wind and storage behind one connection point and promises a delivery profile. If the profile is missed, the developer pays.

What the generator gets in return. A higher tariff than plain solar, often ₹4–5/kWh against ₹2.5–2.7, and a buyer that values every MWh delivered in the hours it needs.

Why it's hard. No single resource can do it. Solar is zero for half the day. Wind in western and southern India collapses from October to February. Storage only moves energy that something else already generated. The design problem is combining all three at the lowest cost that still clears the test in the worst month of the year. Part 2 builds the tools; Part 4 is where to try it.

Part 2 · Utility-scale RE

How projects are bought

Nobody goes looking for a DISCOM to sell to. A central agency announces how much capacity it wants and of what kind, developers bid a price per unit, and the lowest prices win. Most RTC and FDRE capacity is bought this way, through an intermediary that sits between the developer and the DISCOMs.

From tender to signed contractThe sequence every auctioned project goes through. Terms are defined below.
1
RfS issuedThe REIA publishes a Request for Selection: capacity on offer, the product (plain, RTC, FDRE), the test, penalties, and the draft PPA.
2
Bid submittedTechnical and financial envelopes, plus an Earnest Money Deposit. Minimum bid size is usually 50 MW; bidders must show net worth per MW.
3
e-Reverse auctionQualified bidders lower their tariff live, in rounds, until the capacity is filled. The lowest tariffs win, each bidder at its own final price.
4
LoA and PBGWinners get a Letter of Award and post a Performance Bank Guarantee, typically ₹30–50 lakh per MW, which is encashed in stages if the project is late.
5
PSA, then PPAThe REIA signs a Power Sale Agreement with a DISCOM, and only then a back-to-back Power Purchase Agreement with the developer. The regulator adopts the tariff.
6
Build to SCODScheduled commercial operation date, usually 24 months from PPA signing. Part-commissioning is generally allowed.

The intermediary is a Renewable Energy Implementing Agency (REIA): SECI, NTPC, NHPC or SJVN wearing a procurement hat. It signs the Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) with the developer and a mirror-image Power Sale Agreement (PSA) with a DISCOM, keeps a small trading margin (commonly ₹0.07 per unit), and stands between the two. The point of the arrangement is credit. A lender would rather be owed money by a central government company than by a loss-making state DISCOM, and that is what makes a 25-year contract with a shaky buyer financeable.

The catch is sequencing. The PPA only comes alive once a PSA exists, and the REIA cannot sign a PSA until a DISCOM agrees to take the power. Capacity can be auctioned, awarded and then sit for months or years waiting for a buyer; tens of gigawatts were in that state in 2024–25, and some awards were cancelled. It is the single largest risk in the business and it appears again in the risk register.

Two deposits keep bidders honest. The Earnest Money Deposit is bid security, returned to the losers. The Performance Bank Guarantee is posted on award and encashed in stages if the project is late. On the other side, DISCOMs must open a letter of credit, the REIA keeps a payment security fund, and the Late Payment Surcharge Rules of 2022 let generators cut supply to DISCOMs that do not pay, which has sharply reduced overdue bills.

Who signs with whom, and what flowsPick a flow to trace it through the parties.

Delay & termination

Missing SCOD leads to PBG encashment in stages and, beyond a cut-off, reduction of contracted capacity or termination. Extensions are granted for force majeure and for transmission not being ready.

Curtailment

If the grid backs you down for any reason other than grid security, the PPA typically pays you for the energy you would have generated. Backdown for grid security is not compensated. Read the clause; it varies.

Change in law

New duties, taxes or rules after the bid date (basic customs duty on modules, ALMM requirements, GST changes) are passed through, usually as a tariff adjustment spread over the PPA term.

Part 2 · Utility-scale RE

The plain solar PPA, and the hybrid

The simplest contract first. Once its promise and its test are clear, RTC and FDRE are the same idea with the promise made tighter and the test made more frequent.

What a plain solar PPA promises
  • Capacity: a contracted MW (AC) at the point of interconnection. You can install more DC behind it.
  • Energy: whatever you generate, paid at the tariff. No shape is promised.
  • Minimum CUF: you declare an annual CUF at signing (say 24%). Fall more than about 15% below it over a year and you pay compensation on the shortfall. Deliver more than about 10% above it and the excess is bought at a discount, or not at all.
  • Test period: a year. One cloudy week does not matter.
  • Tariff: ₹2.5–2.7/kWh in recent auctions, flat for 25 years.
The wind-solar hybrid
  • Solar and wind behind one connection, often at different sites in the same state.
  • Promises a higher minimum annual CUF, typically 30% or more, because the two resources fill in for each other.
  • Still energy-only and still tested annually. No hour of the day is guaranteed.
  • Tariff ₹3.1–3.5/kWh.
  • This is the stepping stone: it already has the resource mix of an FDRE plant, without the storage or the shape promise.
Plain solar / hybrid test (annual): Actual CUF = energy delivered ÷ (contracted MW × 8,760 h) if Actual CUF < declared CUF × (1 − 15%) → pay shortfall × tariff × multiple if Actual CUF > declared CUF × (1 + 10%) → excess bought at a discount
Oversizing: the habit that carries through

Plants install more module capacity (MWp, DC) than their inverters and connection can export (MW, AC): a DC/AC ratio of 1.4–1.5 at utility scale. The extra modules are wasted at noon, when the inverters clip, and earn their keep in the morning and evening shoulders. Firm products take the same bargain much further: the whole plant, solar plus wind plus storage, is built bigger than the contracted MW, and the surplus exists purely to fill the weak hours.

Why hybrids and firm projects mix the two. Solar peaks at noon and in March–May. Wind peaks in the evening and in the monsoon. Laid over each other they cover much more of the day and the year than either alone, with one stubborn hole: October to February evenings.

P50 and P90

A site's P50 is the output exceeded in half of all years; its P90 is the output exceeded in nine years out of ten. Developers plan on P50 and lenders lend on P90. For a plain solar plant the two differ by a few percent of annual energy. For a firm plant the question is sharper: what is the P90 of the worst month, because that is the month that decides whether penalties are paid.

Part 2 · Utility-scale RE

Storage basics

A battery does not make energy. It is a tank: fill it when energy is cheap and plentiful, empty it when energy is scarce. For an evening-peak contract, that is the entire job.

Power and energy (MW / MWh)

A tank has two sizes, and so does a battery. Power is the width of the pipe, how fast it charges or discharges. Energy is the size of the tank. A "100 MW / 400 MWh" battery delivers 100 MW for four hours; the ratio is its duration (4 h), or in battery jargon 0.25C. The two are chosen separately, for different reasons, and confusing them is the most common sizing mistake in the business.

Round-trip efficiency and usable window

Nothing stored comes back whole. Energy out ÷ energy in, at the grid connection, is 85–88% for a lithium battery and 75–80% for pumped hydro. Every unit shifted into the evening costs about 1.15 units of daytime generation. Operators also keep the battery inside a state-of-charge window of roughly 5–95% to protect the cells, so usable energy is about 90% of nameplate.

Cycles, degradation, augmentation

One full charge and discharge is a cycle; solar-charge, evening-discharge gives 300–360 a year. LFP cells lose 2–3% of capacity each year and are warranted for 6,000–8,000 cycles. The usual answer is augmentation: adding containers around years 8–10 and 16–18. Cheaper than overbuilding on day one, because cell prices keep falling, but only if the layout, cabling and inverters left room for it.

Pumped storage (PSP)

Two reservoirs and reversible turbines. Stores for 6–10 hours, lasts 40 years, takes 4–6 years and a suitable valley to build. Suits load-following contracts with long night deficits, and is more often bought as capacity from a third-party plant than built by the developer.

Sizing rules of thumb
  • Power (MW) ≈ peak demand − firm renewable output at the peak hour in the worst month. For the worked example's 30 MW evening peak, with wind at about 20% CUF in winter, that is roughly 22–27 MW; the example uses 24 MW.

Part 2 · Utility-scale RE

Connection, scheduling and metering

Solar, wind and the battery all meet at one substation and leave through one meter. The size of that doorway, not the size of the plant behind it, is what the contract is written on.

Connectivity and the point of interconnection

The Central Transmission Utility grants the right to inject a fixed MW into the inter-state grid at a named substation, under CERC's General Network Access rules of 2022. Where the plant's line meets the grid is the POI, and the meter sits there. Connectivity normally equals the contracted capacity.

The doorway trick

In the worked example, a 30 MW connection has 50 MW of solar, 35 MW of wind and a 24 MW battery behind it: about 109 MW of equipment. The grid never sees more than 30 MW. Everything extra exists to fill the hours when the resource is weak, and a plant controller caps the output at the limit. This is the core economic idea behind firm renewables.

Transmission charges and the waiver

Using the wires beyond the POI costs a monthly charge per MW, paid by the buyer. Plants commissioned by June 2025 keep a full 25-year waiver; later ones lose 25% a year, and it ends for commissioning after June 2028, with co-located storage protected to 2028. The developer's tariff does not change, but the DISCOM's landed cost does, so the commissioning date is part of the sales pitch.

Scheduling and DSM

The plant tells Grid-India a day ahead what it will inject in each 15-minute block, with revisions during the day. Missing the schedule is charged under the Deviation Settlement Mechanism. A battery is a good schedule-keeper, and good forecasting pays twice: lower deviation charges and more energy delivered when it counts.

The meter

The ABT interface meter at the POI logs every 15-minute block. Every contract test on this page, CUF, availability or DFR, is computed from that log and nothing else.

PPC and EMS

The power plant controller holds injection at the POI on schedule and under the cap. The energy management system decides when the battery charges and discharges, using forecasts and the contract profile. On a firm plant the EMS decides most of the revenue; it deserves to be specified like a generator, not as an afterthought in the SCADA scope.

Part 3 · Firm products

RTC and FDRE defined

Both are promises about MW in particular hours. They differ in which hours, how the promise is measured, and how often the score is settled. Those three choices move the price by more than a rupee a unit.

RTC: round-the-clock

A flat block of contracted MW, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, from renewables plus storage (or, in the 2020 Ministry of Power version, renewables bundled with a coal plant, with renewables at least 51% of the energy). The score is availability: energy delivered as a share of the flat block, added up over a whole year.

Availability(year) = Σ supplied (capped at contract MW) ÷ (contract MW × hours in year) pass if Availability ≥ 80% (RTC-I) shortfall → compensation at a multiple of tariff

A yearly score lets a strong monsoon pay for a weak winter, so the plant can be leaner. That is why early RTC bids cleared far below FDRE: SECI's first round (2020, 400 MW) went at ₹2.90 per unit, rising 3% a year for 15 years.

FDRE: firm and dispatchable RE

Renewables plus storage delivering a profile the buyer writes down: either an assured peak (full capacity in a four-hour evening window, a smaller block the rest of the day) or a load-following profile that tracks the DISCOM's own demand shape. The score is the Demand Fulfilment Ratio (DFR), settled every month.

DFR(month) = Σ supplied in required blocks ÷ Σ demanded in those blocks supplied per block = min(actual injection, demanded) blocks = 15-min (96 per day) pass if DFR ≥ 90% (MoP 2023)

The score is marked block by block, with no extra credit: delivering more than asked in one block never makes up for a gap in another, and each month stands alone. One bad week in November can sink the month. That is what makes FDRE harder, and dearer, than RTC.

Levellised tariff

The flat price per unit that is worth exactly the same, in today's money, as a price that changes over the years. It is how an escalating bid is compared with a flat one, and later on it is how a tariff gets broken into its cost components.

Tariff, shortfall and excess

Revenue on a firm contract is asymmetric. Delivering above the threshold earns the tariff. Missing it costs the lost sale plus a penalty on the gap, often at a multiple of the tariff.

  • Single flat tariff for 25 years. Most FDRE tenders.
  • Peak and off-peak tariffs, with bids ranked on a weighted average. Used in assured-peak designs.
  • Escalating tariff. SECI's RTC-I: ₹2.90 with 3% a year for 15 years, then flat.
  • Excess energy above the profile can go to the procurer at a reduced rate (often around 50–75% of tariff) or be sold on the exchanges. In the models below it is sold at a price you set.
Compensation mechanics
Shortfall (MWh) = max(0, threshold × demanded − supplied) Compensation (₹) = shortfall × tariff × multiple Monthly bill (₹) = supplied × tariff + excess × excess rate − compensation

Some tenders price the shortfall at the market price the buyer paid to replace the power, which can be far higher in evening peaks. Read that clause before bidding.

The worked example: a 30 MW contract served by 50 MW of solar, 35 MW of wind and a 24 MW / 96 MWh battery. The model runs eight weather-varied sample days per month, dispatches the plant hour by hour, settles the monthly test, and feeds the financial model below. Change the mix and watch where it breaks.

How the model dispatches
  1. Solar and wind serve the contracted demand first.
  2. Surplus charges the battery, up to its power and energy limits.
  3. What is left is sold as excess, within the connection headroom (connection = contracted capacity).
  4. Anything beyond the connection is curtailed (wasted).
  5. In a deficit the battery discharges. In assured-peak mode it holds back enough charge for tonight's peak, based on the day's forecast.
  6. Unserved demand is shortfall. Monthly DFR is delivered ÷ demanded over the blocks that count. For RTC the test is the annual total.

Simplifications
  • Hourly steps instead of 15-minute blocks, so intra-hour wind ramps are smoothed out.
  • Synthetic resource profiles shaped on Indian sites. Real bids use P50/P90 8,760-hour series from measured data.
  • Battery usable energy is 92% of nameplate. No auxiliary load or transformer losses.
  • Degradation is held flat because the finance model pays for augmentation instead.
  • FDRE peak mode tests DFR in peak hours only. Many tenders also test off-peak blocks.

Financing basics for utility-scale

In an auction the tariff is the unknown. Everything else about the project is fixed first, and then the question is: what is the lowest price per unit at which this plant still pays its lenders and its owners? A few ideas are enough to answer it.

Capex per MW

The ruler for everything. In 2025–26, roughly ₹2.3–2.8 crore per MWp of solar, ₹6–7 crore per MW of wind, ₹1.0–1.3 crore per MWh of battery, ₹1–1.5 crore per MW of connection. Because a firm plant is so much bigger than its contract, the worked example costs about ₹18–19 crore per contracted MW, roughly four times a plain solar plant.

Debt, equity, tenor

Projects run on 70–75% debt from IREDA, PFC, REC and banks at 8.5–9.5%, repaid over 18–21 years, with the developer's equity making up the rest. Tenor matters more than it looks: a longer loan means a smaller repayment each year, and a smaller repayment means a lower tariff can carry it.

DSCR

Lenders judge each year by one ratio: cash after operating costs and tax ÷ that year's interest plus principal. Never below about 1.20, averaging around 1.30, checked in the pessimistic P90 case with a weak-wind year and its penalties. It is the binding limit on how much debt a project can carry.

Project IRR and equity IRR

The return on the whole project's cash flows before financing, and the return on the owner's cash after the lenders are paid. Developers bid to a target equity IRR, typically 12–15% after tax.

WACC and levellised cost

The blended cost of debt and equity. Discount every year's cost and every year's energy at the WACC, divide one by the other, and you have a levellised cost per unit. That is how the tariff build-up chart is made.

How the bid is solved

Where the bid is won. Capex and cost of capital set most of the tariff, but on FDRE the configuration matters as much. An oversized plant with a smaller battery and good excess-sale revenue can beat a lean plant that leaks penalties every winter.

Engineering checklist
  • 8,760-hour (or 15-min) yield and dispatch model on P50 and P90 series
  • Monthly DFR by year, including degradation
  • Grid studies for connectivity and compliance
  • BESS layout with augmentation plinths
  • Auxiliary power and transformer losses in the energy model
  • Cable and transformer sizing for future containers
Commissioning sequence
  • Pooling substation and line energised first
  • Solar and wind blocks in tranches (part-COD)
  • BESS capacity and round-trip efficiency tests
  • PPC step-response, ramp and reactive tests
  • EMS profile-tracking trial over several days
  • Grid-code compliance certification and COD declaration
Contract terms to push for
  • Performance guarantees tied to tested capacity and RTE, not to DFR, which depends on weather
  • Clear interface responsibility for EMS logic
  • Delay LDs capped and aligned with the PPA's PBG schedule
  • Augmentation scope and pricing agreed up front
  • Change-in-law pass-through for ALMM and duties

Market timeline

How India got from plain solar auctions to firm, dispatchable RE. Tariffs are indicative.

2010–17Solar auctions drive tariffs from ₹12 to under ₹2.5/kWh

Reverse auctions, falling module prices and the ISTS waiver make plain solar the cheapest new power in India.

2018National Wind-Solar Hybrid Policy

MNRE's framework for co-located and virtual hybrids. Hybrid tenders with minimum annual CUF follow.

2020First RTC and assured-peak auctions

SECI's RTC-I (400 MW, 80% annual availability) clears at ₹2.90/kWh with 3% escalation for 15 years. SECI's assured-peak tender introduces separate peak and off-peak pricing, with weighted tariffs around ₹4/kWh.

2020MoP RTC guidelines (RE bundled with thermal)

Allow RE to be bundled with coal to supply RTC power, with RE at least 51% of energy and 85% availability.

2022GNA Regulations and LPS Rules

CERC's General Network Access regime reworks connectivity. The Late Payment Surcharge Rules tighten DISCOM payment discipline.

2023MoP FDRE guidelines

Standard bidding framework for firm and dispatchable RE with storage. DFR of at least 90% on a monthly basis, with assured-peak and load-following variants.

2023–25FDRE auctions scale up

SJVN, NHPC, SECI and NTPC run FDRE rounds clearing roughly ₹4–5/kWh. Standalone BESS tenders push storage prices down sharply. A growing pile of awarded capacity waits on PSAs.

2025ISTS waiver taper begins

Projects commissioned after 30 June 2025 get a partial waiver, stepping down 25% a year, while co-located storage keeps its waiver to 2028.

2026ALMM List-II for cells

Domestic cell requirements kick in for covered projects, raising module costs and making change-in-law clauses more important.
